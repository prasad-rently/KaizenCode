⚖️ The Referee

A referee doesn't play the game — they hold everyone to the same rules, consistently, and make the hard calls out in the open. Performance management is the part of leading people where "be liked" and "be fair" collide hardest. Done well, it's not about catching people out; it's about making expectations so clear, and standards so even, that no one is ever surprised by where they stand.

Fourth module of the Leadership track. No runnable code — instead: a level ladder, a skill/will diagnostic, decision scenarios, and a builder for this module's deliverable: a career ladder for engineers L3–L6.

1Fairness, not friendship — the no-surprises promise

Performance management is where new managers freeze, because it puts their need to be liked in direct tension with their duty to be fair. Your job here isn't to be everyone's friend; it's to be the one person who applies the same standard to everyone, visibly, and tells the truth about it early.

Everything in this module serves one promise: no one should ever be surprised. Not by a review rating, not by a low bonus, and above all not by being let go. A surprise means you failed to give feedback in time — the outcome may be right, but you delivered it cruelly. If a review or a firing shocks someone, that's a bug in your process, not theirs.

2The career ladder — what "good" means at each level

You can't hold someone to a bar they can't see. A career ladder writes the bar down: what's expected at each level, in behaviors and impact rather than vibes. The single most important idea in any ladder — and the one people get wrong — is what actually rises as you climb:

↑ seniorbigger scope · more ambiguity · less direction
L3
Engineer — delivers well-scoped tasks reliably, with guidance. Scope: a task. Needs the "what"; owns the "how" of small work.
L4
Senior Engineer — owns features end-to-end, independently. Scope: a project. Owns the "how"; needs only the "what" framed.
L5
Staff Engineer — drives ambiguous, cross-cutting work; a force multiplier. Scope: a team's domain. Finds the right "what" too.
L6
Principal Engineer — sets technical direction for an org. Scope: an org. Creates clarity from ambiguity for others.

Notice what does not define the ladder: "writes better code." A senior engineer isn't just a faster junior — they operate at a larger scope of impact with less direction. You'll design a full version of this in the deliverable below.

The dual ladder. From L5 up, the path forks: IC (Staff → Principal) and Management (Manager → Director) are equal in seniority, different in the work. Don't force your best engineers into management to "get promoted" — that loses a great builder and often makes a reluctant manager.

3Expectations are set forward, not discovered

The ladder is only useful if people know where they stand against it before review season, not as a verdict at the end. Your ongoing job is to connect today's work to the level: "Owning this project cleanly is exactly the L4 behavior — do it a few more times and that's your case." Or, honestly: "To hit L5 you'd need to be driving the direction here, not just executing it. Right now you're executing it well."

Set the target forward, name the gap specifically, and revisit it in your 1:1s (that's what The Gardener's growth-focus line is for). A promotion should be the documentation of a level someone is already operating at — never a gamble that they'll grow into it.

4Calibration — making a rating mean the same thing everywhere

Left alone, every manager rates differently. Some are lenient, some harsh; everyone is prone to the halo effect (one great trait colors everything), recency bias (last month outweighs the year), and affinity bias (people like me score higher). The result: an "exceeds" from one manager and an "exceeds" from another mean nothing in common — and someone's raise depends on whose team they landed on.

Calibration is the fix: managers put their ratings side by side and defend each one against the same ladder, with evidence. A rating only survives if the person's actual work matches what that level describes. It's uncomfortable — you sometimes have to lower your own report's rating — but it's the machinery that makes fairness real instead of rhetorical.

The test for any rating: could you show a stranger the ladder and the evidence, and would they land on the same score? If it only makes sense because you know and like the person, it's not calibrated — it's biased.

5Diagnose before you act — skill vs will

When someone underperforms, the reflex is to jump to consequences. Don't — first find out why, because the fix is completely different for each cause. A fast, honest diagnostic is skill (can they?) against will (do they want to?):

▲ high will
Low skill · high will

Coach & train. Eager but not yet able. Teach, pair, give time and stretch — the most rewarding quadrant.

High skill · high will

Delegate & stretch. Your stars. Give autonomy and bigger problems, then get out of the way.

Low skill · low will

The hard road. Often a fit or role problem. Direct feedback, a real chance — and honesty if it doesn't turn.

High skill · low will

Re-motivate. Capable but checked out — usually autonomy, purpose, or a life reason. Find the cause first.

▼ low will　　　　low skill ◀　　　▶ high skill

Also check context before concluding it's the person: unclear goals, a bad project, a blocker you haven't cleared, or something personal outside work. Plenty of "performance problems" are really management problems or temporary life storms in disguise. Ask, with genuine curiosity, before you reach for a process.

6The PIP — an honest path back, not a paper trail

A Performance Improvement Plan is one of the most abused tools in management. Done honestly, it's a clear, time-boxed, winnable plan: specific goals, the support to hit them, and a genuine belief the person can. Done dishonestly, it's box-ticking cover for a decision already made — a cruelty dressed as a chance.

❌ Sham PIP

Outcome already decided
Vague or impossible goals
No real support offered
First they've heard of the problem
Theater to satisfy HR / the paper trail

✅ Honest PIP

You genuinely want them to pass
Specific, measurable, achievable goals
Concrete support & check-ins
Follows months of clear feedback
A real, fair chance to turn it around

If the honest truth is that the decision is already made, a PIP is the wrong instrument — run a respectful exit instead of a fake process. And a PIP should never be someone's first news that they're struggling; if it is, you skipped step five and the no-surprises promise.

7Letting someone go — with dignity

Sometimes, after honest feedback and a real chance, it still isn't working. Then the kindest thing — for the person, and for the team carrying them — is a clean, respectful exit. How you do it defines you as a leader, because the whole team is watching how you treat someone on the way out, and quietly deciding how safe they are.

  • No surprises. They should already know it's a real possibility from prior feedback. The meeting confirms; it doesn't ambush.
  • Be direct and brief. Say it clearly in the first minute — don't bury it in a soft-shoe preamble that makes them relax first. Kindness is clarity, not padding.
  • Own the decision. "This is my decision," not "HR made me." Don't outsource the responsibility to protect your own comfort.
  • Preserve their dignity. In private, never in anger, with as much generosity as you can (severance, an honest reference, a graceful story to tell). They're still a person with a life.
The opposite failure is just as real: keeping a clearly-failing person out of conflict-avoidance. It's unfair to the teammates silently covering for them, unfair to the person denied the honest feedback that could help them elsewhere, and it quietly tells your best people that the bar is optional. Not acting is a decision — usually the wrong one.
