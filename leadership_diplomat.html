🕊️ The Diplomat

A diplomat commands no army. They get things done across borders they don't control — through conversation, understanding, and trust. As you rise, more and more of your work looks like this: moving peers who don't report to you, defusing conflicts before they fester, and having the hard conversations most people spend enormous energy avoiding. The avoidance is the real cost. This is how to stop paying it.

Fifth module of the Leadership track. No runnable code — instead: the anatomy of a hard conversation, an interests-vs-positions contrast, decision scenarios, and a builder for this module's deliverable: role-play scripts for three hard conversations.

1Your power is conversation, not command

Early on, work gets done because you do it or because you can assign it. Higher up, the most important things depend on people you can't tell what to do: a peer manager, another team, a skeptical stakeholder, an exec, your own boss. Authority runs out fast; influence doesn't. The leaders who scale are the ones who can walk into a room with no formal power and leave with alignment.

Two skills carry almost all of it, and both are learnable: having the hard conversation instead of avoiding it, and negotiating from interests instead of fighting over positions. Everything below is one of those two.

2The hard conversation — avoided until it explodes

The default move with a hard conversation is to postpone it — soften it, hint at it in Slack, hope it self-resolves. It rarely does; it compounds. The unspoken problem grows, resentment sets in, and by the time you finally address it, it's ten times harder and the other person feels ambushed. The kindest, cheapest time to have it is early. There's a repeatable shape:

the shape of a hard conversation →
1
Prepare — what's the real issue, and what outcome do you actually want? Not "vent" — a goal.
2
Open directly — name it in the first minute, kindly. No long warm-up that makes them relax before the drop.
3
State the facts — SBI, not labels; then stop and let it land. "Here's what I observed," not "you always…".
4
Listen — genuinely understand their side. This is the real work — most of the conversation.
5
Close — agree one concrete change and a check-in. End with a path, not just a problem.

Notice steps 3 and 4 are the opposite of what anxiety wants you to do — anxiety wants you to talk more to fill the silence. State your facts cleanly, then get quiet and hand them the floor.

3Actually listening — the most underrated skill

Most people don't listen; they wait to talk. In a hard conversation or a negotiation, that's fatal — because people who feel genuinely heard become reasonable, and people who don't, dig in. Feeling understood is often what someone wants more than winning. Real listening is a set of concrete moves:

  • Don't interrupt, and leave a beat of silence. The most useful thing is often said right after the pause you were tempted to fill.
  • Reflect it back. "So what I'm hearing is you're worried this ships broken and lands on you — is that right?" It proves you listened and lets them correct you.
  • Separate hearing from agreeing. You can fully understand someone's view without conceding it. "I get why you see it that way" is not "you're right."
  • Ask, then be quiet. "What's your read?" followed by real silence beats any clever argument.

4Interests, not positions

The single most useful idea in negotiation (from Getting to Yes): a position is what someone says they want; an interest is why they want it. Positions are demands, and demands collide. Interests are needs, and needs can often be met more than one way. Argue positions and you get a stalemate or a grudging split; uncover interests and you find room nobody saw.

Position — what they say

"Move the deadline!"
"I need three more engineers."
"We have to rewrite it in Go."
"Reject this whole design."

Interest — what they need

"I'm scared we ship broken & I'm blamed."
"My team is drowning and burning out."
"I need it to scale and stop paging me."
"I don't trust that it's safe under load."

The classic story: two people fight over one orange and agree to cut it in half — only to discover one wanted the juice and the other wanted the peel for baking. Split down the middle, each got half of what they needed. Had they asked why, each could have had all of it. So the diplomat's habit is the question: "Help me understand — what are you actually trying to protect here?"

5BATNA — your quiet source of power

Your real leverage in any negotiation isn't how hard you push — it's your BATNA: your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. It's what you'll do if you walk away with no deal. A strong walk-away option lets you hold the line calmly; a weak one means you'll accept almost anything, and the other side can feel it.

  • Know yours before you enter. "If we don't agree, I'll ___." If that alternative is decent, you can negotiate from ease instead of desperation.
  • Estimate theirs. Their willingness to move depends on how good their walk-away is. Someone with no other option will bend further than they let on.
  • Improve yours quietly. The best way to win a negotiation is often done beforehand — line up a second option so you genuinely don't need this one.
  • Never accept worse than your BATNA. If the deal on the table is worse than walking away, walk away. That's the entire point of knowing it.

6Mediating conflict — don't play telephone

When two of your people are in conflict, the trap is becoming the switchboard: each vents to you separately and asks you to fix the other. Relaying messages back and forth (the "telephone game") is exhausting, distorts everything, and lets both avoid the actual conversation — which just entrenches the fight.

  • Get them in a room together, with you facilitating — not carrying messages between two rooms.
  • Each states their view and is heard (reflect it back) before anyone rebuts. Half of most conflict evaporates once each person truly feels understood.
  • Separate the people from the problem. Frame it as "the two of you vs the shared problem," not "you vs each other." Find the goal they both actually want.
  • Agree on specific future behaviors, not an apology for the past. "Next time X, we'll do Y."
Don't rush to declare a winner. Picking a side and telling the other to adjust ends the meeting but not the conflict — it just teaches the "loser" to route around you. Your job is to help them resolve it, not to referee a verdict onto them.

7Influence without authority

Most of what you'll need as you grow, you can't order — you have to earn. Influence without authority is a craft with real moves:

  • Trade in currencies they value. People help those who help them. Understand the other person's goals and pressures, and frame your ask as something that also advances theirs — or offer something they need in return.
  • Build the relationship before you need it. The time to bank goodwill is before the ask. A cold "can you drop everything for me?" from a stranger loses to a warm one from someone who's helped them before.
  • Make it easy to say yes. Do the thinking, bring a crisp proposal, reduce their effort to a nod. Vague, high-effort asks get deprioritized.
  • Manage up the same way. Your boss is a stakeholder too: bring solutions not just problems, make their life easier, and give them no surprises. Managing up well is what buys you autonomy.
Escalation is a tool, not a first move. Going over someone's head to force a decision burns the relationship you'll need next quarter. Try to solve it peer-to-peer first; escalate openly ("I think we should get our leads in a room") rather than secretly, and only when direct diplomacy has genuinely stalled.

9Deliverable — script three hard conversations

The muscle is built in reps, and a little scripting beforehand turns dread into a plan. Draft a script for one hard conversation you're avoiding — the generator gives you a full model script from sensible defaults — then run it for three different situations (a feedback talk, a peer conflict, a no to a stakeholder):

Who & the situation
My goal for this conversation (an outcome, not "to vent")
The facts I'll state (SBI — specific, no labels)
What I most need to understand from them
My walk-away / bottom line if nothing changes
Try it: (1) Work through all five scenarios — notice how many traps are just an avoided conversation or a fight over a position. (2) Generate a script and rehearse the opening line out loud until it's easy. (3) For your next negotiation, write down your BATNA and one guess at the other side's real interest before the meeting. (4) Do it three times, for three different hard conversations — that's the deliverable.
