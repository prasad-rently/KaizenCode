💎 The Treasury

If your company takes venture money, then equity — how it's raised, split, and diluted — quietly governs your own net worth, your team's, and half the incentives around you. As a CTO you'll sit in investor diligence, hold meaningful shares, and help decide when and how to raise. The founders who understand cap tables keep more of their company; the ones who don't get a nasty surprise at signing. This is the math nobody teaches engineers.

Final module of Stage 4 · Business Fluency. The centerpiece is a real cap-table dilution simulator — model founder ownership from founding through Series B, and watch % fall while value climbs.

1Why a CTO learns venture math

Equity is the currency of startups, and most engineers treat it as a mystery number in an offer letter. As a leader you can't afford that. You'll be granting equity to your team (and fielding their questions), sitting across from investors who do this for a living, and making decisions — how much to raise, at what price — that determine what everyone's shares are worth. Financial fluency (from The Counting House) covers the operating business; this covers how the company is owned and financed.

2How a round works — pre-money, post-money, dilution

A funding round has a simple core. The pre-money valuation is what the company is worth before the new money goes in; add the raise and you get the post-money valuation. The new investors' ownership is just raise ÷ post-money. Raise $5M at a $20M pre-money → $25M post → investors own 5/25 = 20%.

That 20% comes from somewhere: everyone who already owned shares. This is dilution — every round shrinks your percentage. But here's the mindset shift that separates good founders from anxious ones:

❌ The dilution trap (chase %)

Raise as little as possible
Guard your ownership % above all
Fear every new investor
A big slice is the goal
Optimize the percentage

✅ The value view (chase $)

Raise what the plan actually needs
% falls, but the pie grows faster
Great investors add value, not just cash
A small slice of a huge company wins
Optimize the stake's worth

A founder who owns 100% of a company worth nothing has nothing. A founder who owns 40% of a company worth $150M has $60M. Dilution is the price of building something big — the calculator below makes that trade concrete.

4SAFEs & the option pool — the two things that surprise people

Two mechanics catch first-time founders off guard, and both quietly cost them:

  • SAFEs & convertible notes. Early money often comes in as a SAFE — a promise to convert to equity at the next priced round, usually at a valuation cap and/or discount. The surprise: if you raised on a $5M cap and later price the round at $20M, those SAFEs convert as if the company were worth $5M — so they take a much bigger slice than the dollars suggest. Always model the fully-diluted cap table including SAFE conversion before you sign the priced round.
  • The option-pool shuffle. Investors will require an option pool (for future hires) be created pre-money — which means it comes out of the founders' and existing holders' shares, not the new investors'. A "20% pre-money pool" is a stealth dilution of you. Negotiate the pool to what you'll actually grant before the next round (usually ~10%), not an inflated number that pads the investor's effective ownership.
The option pool is where your team's equity comes from. As CTO, that pool funds every engineer's grant. Fight for a healthy pool — but understand each grant dilutes the founders and everyone else, so it's a real budget to steward, not free money.

5What investors ask a CTO in diligence — and debt vs equity

When a round gets serious, investors run technical due diligence, and you're the one in the chair. They're probing for the risks that could sink their investment. Be ready with honest answers and mitigations:

  • Scalability & architecture — will this hold up at 10× the load, or is a rewrite looming?
  • Tech debt & code quality — how much of the roadmap is really achievable, or is it mortgaged to shortcuts?
  • Security & compliance — SOC 2, data handling, past incidents (the questionnaires from The Bazaar live here too).
  • Key-person / bus-factor risk — "what happens if your lead architect leaves?" Documentation and cross-training are the answer that keeps a deal alive.
  • IP ownership — did every contractor and founder actually assign their IP to the company? A gap here can kill a deal.

Debt vs equity, briefly: equity is patient but expensive (you give up ownership forever). Venture debt is a loan — it extends runway without dilution, but it must be repaid and is a claim on the company's assets, so it adds risk. The right use is to reach a milestone that de-risks the next equity round — not to avoid a raise you genuinely need.

6Pressure-test your instincts

Equity thinking fails in predictable ways — fearing dilution over value, missing the pool shuffle, ignoring SAFE overhang, and fumbling diligence. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run the cap-table simulator — notice founders drop from 100% to ~40% while their stake grows from nothing to tens of millions. (2) If your company has raised, ask to see the real cap table and find where the option pool and any SAFEs sit. (3) For your own equity grant, work out your fully-diluted % and what it's worth at the current valuation. (4) Write down an honest answer to "what happens if your key engineer leaves?" — then go reduce that risk.
