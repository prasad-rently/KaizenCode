📱 Silent Push Notifications: Opportunities, Not Guarantees

How background push really behaves on iOS and Android — and why treating it as a reliable delivery channel is one of the most common mobile mistakes.

Mobile Development · ~8 min read · updated August 2026

TL;DR — A silent push wakes your app in the background with no UI so it can quietly refresh. It's a wonderful optimisation and a terrible guarantee: the OS throttles it hard, and it simply won't arrive when the app is force-quit, the battery is low, or background refresh is off. Design it as a hint to sync, never as the thing that carries your data.

1 · What a silent notification actually is

A normal push is a doorbell — it lights the screen, plays a sound, shows text. A silent (or background) push is the opposite: a quiet tap on your app's shoulder. No banner, no sound, no badge. The user never sees it. Instead the operating system wakes your app for a few seconds in the background so it can do a small job — fetch new messages, refresh a badge count, pre-download content so the next screen opens instantly.

That's the appeal: your app can feel "always fresh" without the user doing anything. The catch — which the rest of this article is about — is that the platforms treat this power as a favour they can revoke at any moment.

2 · What makes a push "silent" (iOS / APNs)

On iOS, a push is silent when its payload carries content-available: 1 and omits alert, sound, and badge:

APNs payload · JSON
{
  "aps": {
    "content-available": 1
  },
  // your own keys — keep them tiny; treat them as a "go sync" flag,
  // not as the payload itself (see §7)
  "type": "inbox-changed"
}

Two request headers are just as important as the body, and since iOS 13 they're mandatory for background pushes — get them wrong and APNs rejects or mis-handles the message:

APNs request · shell
# apns-push-type MUST be "background"; apns-priority MUST be 5.
# (priority 10 is for visible alerts and is rejected for background.)
curl -v \
  -H "apns-push-type: background" \
  -H "apns-priority: 5" \
  -H "apns-topic: com.example.app" \
  -H "authorization: bearer <JWT>" \
  -d '{"aps":{"content-available":1},"type":"inbox-changed"}' \
  https://api.push.apple.com/3/device/<device-token>
Priority matters. Background pushes must be sent at apns-priority: 5. It tells the system "this can wait for a good moment" — which is exactly why the OS feels free to delay, batch, or drop it. High priority (10) is reserved for pushes the user will see.

3 · What your app has to do to receive one

Three pieces have to line up:

  • Capability: enable the Remote notifications background mode (UIBackgroundModes → remote-notification on iOS).
  • A handler that the system calls when the push arrives.
  • A completion callback you must call when you're done.
iOS · Swift
func application(
  _ application: UIApplication,
  didReceiveRemoteNotification userInfo: [AnyHashable: Any],
  fetchCompletionHandler completion: @escaping (UIBackgroundFetchResult) -> Void
) {
  Task {
    let changed = await store.syncFromServer()   // small, fast unit of work
    // You get ~30 seconds. ALWAYS call completion — if you don't,
    // iOS may kill the app and throttle your future silent pushes.
    completion(changed ? .newData : .noData)
  }
}
React Native / cross-platform: the same rules apply underneath. Libraries expose a background handler (e.g. a "background message" callback) — the OS constraints below don't change because you're in JavaScript. Keep the work small and finish quickly.

4 · The uncomfortable truth: best-effort, not guaranteed

Here's the part that surprises most people. Apple dynamically throttles background pushes to protect battery, data, and performance. There is no contract that your silent push will ever run.

  • Rule of thumb: don't count on more than 2–3 per hour per device; send them in bursts or too frequently and the extras are simply dropped.
  • The system weighs device state, how often the user actually opens your app, and overall conditions before deciding to wake you at all.
  • Even when delivered, timing is the OS's choice — it may coalesce several into one, or hold them until the phone is charging or on Wi-Fi.
Never use silent push for real-time or must-arrive data. Chat delivery, trades, live location, "your ride is here" — none of these should depend on a silent push. Use a live connection (WebSocket/SSE) for real-time, and a visible notification when the message must reach the user.

5 · When silent pushes just… don't show up

Beyond throttling, there are conditions where a background push is dropped outright, with no error you can see:

ConditionWhat happens
User force-quit the app (swiped it away)iOS stops delivering background pushes to it entirely, until the user opens it again.
Low Power Mode is onBackground activity is suspended; silent pushes are held or dropped.
Background App Refresh disabled (per-app or global)The app won't be woken in the background at all.
Poor / no connectivityAPNs holds only the most recent one, and only for a limited window.
App rarely usedThe system deprioritises waking apps the user has ignored.

The lesson: a silent push that doesn't arrive looks exactly like one that was never sent. Your system has to be correct either way.

6 · Android is the same story, different words

On Android the analogue is an FCM data-only message (no notification block, just a data payload). It faces its own gauntlet: Doze mode and App Standby batch or defer background work, there are hard limits on background execution, and many device makers layer aggressive battery managers on top. High-priority data messages can wake the device, but abusing that gets your app flagged. Same conclusion: helpful, not guaranteed.

7 · How to design around it (the pattern that works)

Every reliable system treats the silent push as a nudge, not a delivery:

  • Send a flag, not the data. The push says "something changed — come and get it." The app then pulls the actual state from your API. If the push is lost, the next launch (or a periodic fetch) reconciles anyway.
  • Make the sync idempotent. Because pushes get coalesced and retried, "sync now" must be safe to run zero, one, or five times with the same result.
  • Reconcile on foreground. Always re-sync when the app comes to the foreground; that's your guaranteed catch-up path when background wakes were throttled.
  • Pair with the right primary channel. Real-time → WebSocket/SSE while foregrounded. Must-notify-the-user → a visible push. Silent push is the optimisation layer that makes things feel instant when it happens to fire.
  • Keep the work tiny. You have seconds. Fetch a delta, not the world, and finish the completion handler fast.
Mental model: think of silent push like a colleague who sometimes remembers to tell you the docs changed. Great when it happens — but you still check the shared drive yourself before you rely on being current. Build the system so it's correct when the reminder never comes.

8 · Quick checklist

  • ✅ content-available: 1, no alert/sound/badge · headers apns-push-type: background + apns-priority: 5.
  • ✅ Background mode enabled; completion handler always called within the window.
  • ✅ Payload is a small "go sync" flag; real data comes from your API.
  • ✅ Sync is idempotent and also runs on foreground.
  • ❌ Don't send more than a few per hour, or expect delivery when force-quit / Low Power / refresh-off.
  • ❌ Don't put anything time-critical or user-facing solely behind a silent push.

9 · Key takeaways

Silent push is one of mobile's best opportunities — a way to keep an app fresh and fast for free — precisely because the platform is free to ignore it. Lean into it as an optimisation, back it with a channel you actually control, and your app stays correct whether the quiet tap on the shoulder arrives or not.
