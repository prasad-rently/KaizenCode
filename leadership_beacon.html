📡 The Beacon

Nobody applies to a company they've never heard of, and no engineering org builds a reputation by accident. Public presence — the blog, the conference talk, the well-judged post — is a compounding asset: every piece of real technical writing keeps working long after you hit publish, pulling in candidates and credibility while you sleep. It's also the executive skill most engineers never practice, because writing for strangers feels riskier than writing for a pull request.

Sixth module of Stage 5 · The CTO Craft. The centerpiece is a real technical post outline builder — turn a piece of real engineering work into a structured draft, and the deliverable is publishing it.

1Why public presence is a CTO job, not a nice-to-have

A strong engineering brand does three things no amount of internal-only excellence can: it makes hiring cheaper (inbound candidates who already respect the work convert far better than cold outreach), it makes the CTO's word carry further externally (investors, partners, the press all weight public credibility), and it attracts the kind of engineers who read engineering blogs — often your best hires. None of this requires a marketing department; it requires a CTO willing to occasionally write and speak in public, and a habit of doing it consistently rather than in bursts.

2The engineering blog — what actually works

Most engineering blogs fail for the same reason most technical talks fail: they're written to sound impressive instead of to be useful. The posts that get shared and remembered follow a simple shape:

✅ Posts that spread

A specific problem, with real numbers
The wrong approaches tried first, honestly
One clear insight a reader can reuse
Concrete code/diagrams, not just prose
A narrow, honest title over a grand one

⚠️ Posts that don't

"We migrated to microservices" with no numbers
Reads like a press release, not an engineer
No mistakes admitted — implausibly smooth
Buried lede — the insight is in paragraph 9
Written once, then the blog goes dark for a year

Cadence matters more than polish: a blog that publishes something real every month or two, even short, beats one that publishes a masterpiece annually. Consistency is what turns a blog into a habit readers (and candidates) come back to.

3Conference talks & dev-rel

A conference talk is a blog post with a harder deadline and a live audience — the same "specific problem, honest mistakes, one reusable insight" shape applies, but a talk also builds something writing can't: a room full of people who now recognize your face and your company's name. Dev-rel — open-sourcing tools, sponsoring or speaking at meetups, answering in public forums — is the same flywheel at smaller, more frequent scale. Don't treat these as all-or-nothing: one well-prepared talk a year, done well, outperforms five rushed, forgettable ones.

4Hiring through visibility

The flywheel is simple and slow to start: real technical content → engineers discover and respect the work → inbound interest rises → the recruiting funnel gets cheaper and higher-quality → the company can afford to be more selective, which raises the bar, which produces more content worth writing about. It compounds over quarters, not weeks — the mistake is expecting a single post to move a hiring pipeline, then abandoning the habit when it doesn't. Judge it over a year, not a launch week.

5Social-media judgment for executives

Once you're a public technical face of the company, everything you post is read as company position whether you intend that or not. A few durable rules: separate opinion from company position explicitly when the topic is remotely contentious; never post about an active incident before the crisis-comms plan has cleared it — see The Storm Keeper; don't reveal competitive or customer specifics even in an otherwise harmless technical post — see The Fine Print on confidentiality; and assume permanence — a post can be deleted, a screenshot cannot.

6Deliverable — outline a real technical post

Pick a real piece of engineering work — a problem you solved, a mistake you learned from, a system you built — and the builder turns it into a structured, publish-ready outline. Fill it in, then actually write and publish it:

The problem — specific, with real numbers if you have them
What you tried first that didn't work
What actually fixed it
The one reusable insight for the reader
Working title

7Pressure-test your instincts

Public presence rewards honesty and consistency, and punishes polish without substance and posts written faster than they're thought through. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Use the outline builder above on a real piece of your own engineering work, then actually publish it — internally at minimum, publicly if you can. (2) Read your last three planned social posts as if you were a competitor or a journalist — what would they take from it? (3) If your engineering blog has gone quiet, commit to one honest post this month rather than a big relaunch. (4) Before your next public talk, cut the slide count in half and add one honest failure you haven't shared before.
