🏛️ The Magistrate

Every acquisition closes on a story, but it's built on what technical due diligence actually finds. As a CTO you'll sit on both sides of this table across your career — running DD on a company you're acquiring, and surviving DD on the company you built. Both roles reward the same instinct: look for the truth under the pitch, calmly, before the wire transfer makes it permanent.

Fourth module of Stage 5 · The CTO Craft. The centerpiece is a real due-diligence checklist — score each item and get an honest go / caution / halt read on your own project.

1Why technical DD decides deals

Financial and legal diligence get most of the attention in a deal, but technical diligence is what tells you whether the thing you're buying (or the thing you're being bought for) actually works the way the pitch says it does. A clean cap table doesn't help you if the codebase is unmaintainable, the "proprietary AI" is three engineers babysitting a prompt, or the team that built it all leaves the day the earn-out clears. Technical DD exists to answer one question for the acquirer, and to prepare one answer for the target: is what's being described what's actually there?

2Running DD as the acquirer

A good technical DD covers six areas — go deep enough to be honest, not so deep you kill the deal timeline on things that don't matter:

ArchitectureDoes it scale to where the combined company needs it to go, or does the pitch deck's growth story break the system a year in? Read the actual bottlenecks, not the roadmap slide.
Code & debtTest coverage, deploy frequency, incident history, and how much of "velocity" is masking a debt bill someone will have to pay. A demo always works; ask what breaks under real load.
SecurityPast breaches, pen-test history, how secrets and access are managed. A single undisclosed prior breach can be a deal-killer on its own — see below.
IP & licensingDoes the company actually own its code? Chase unassigned contractor IP and copyleft license exposure — this is The Fine Print's territory, and it belongs in every DD checklist.
TeamWho actually holds the architecture in their head, and are they staying? Key-person risk is invisible in an org chart and decisive in an integration.
Vendor & costHidden infra spend, vendor lock-in, and contracts that don't survive a change of control (some MSAs let the vendor walk or reprice on acquisition).
Weight the checklist, don't just tally it. A dozen minor findings rarely kill a deal; one severe finding — undisclosed breach, no IP assignment on core code, a single engineer who is the entire system's tribal knowledge — usually should. Diligence isn't a scorecard average, it's a search for the one thing that changes the price or the decision.

3Surviving DD as the target

The best time to prepare for diligence is long before anyone asks for it. The teams that come through clean aren't the ones with nothing wrong — they're the ones who found their own landmines first:

  • Run your own audit before they do. Everything in the acquirer's checklist above, you should already know the answer to. A surprise the acquirer finds costs you leverage; a surprise you disclose yourself costs you almost nothing.
  • Fix what's cheap to fix now. An unsigned contractor IP assignment takes a signature today and a deal-threatening negotiation during diligence. Close known gaps months before a deal is even on the table.
  • Document the architecture, not just the code. If the system's design lives only in one engineer's head, diligence reads that as risk regardless of how good the code actually is. Write it down.
  • Be straight about what's bad. A target who disclosed their tech debt honestly and has a credible plan reads as trustworthy; a target whose debt surfaces mid-diligence reads as either dishonest or unaware — both are worse than the debt itself.

4Integration and acquihires

The deal closing is the easy part; making two engineering orgs and two stacks work as one is where most of the real risk lives — and it's usually underweighted at signing:

✅ Integration done well

A named integration owner and a real 100-day plan
Systems merge on a deliberate timeline, not "day one"
Retention offers signed before close, not after
Cultural/process gaps named, not assumed away

⚠️ Integration done badly

"We'll figure out the merge after close"
Forcing a stack rewrite immediately post-acquisition
Key engineers leave once their retention window ends
Two teams, two tools, no decision on which wins

An acquihire — buying a company mainly for its team, not its product or revenue — flips the checklist above: the product and architecture matter far less than whether the specific people you're paying for will actually stay, and for how long. Structure retention (vesting, milestones) as the real center of that deal, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

Rate each item: Clear (no concern), Watch (minor, fixable), or Red flag (severe — would change the price or the decision).

6What actually kills deals

Most findings are negotiated into the price. A short list of things tend to kill the deal outright, or should: undisclosed prior breaches, no IP assignment on core revenue-generating code, financial or usage metrics that don't reconcile with the pitch, and key engineers who make clear in diligence interviews that they're leaving regardless of retention terms. If you find one of these, the conversation isn't "how do we price this in" — it's "should this deal happen at all."

7Pressure-test your instincts

Diligence rewards patience and honesty on both sides of the table, and punishes rushing past what a finding is actually telling you. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run the checklist above against your own project — honestly, not optimistically — and see what the verdict says. (2) If you're ever on the target side, write your own disclosure memo before anyone asks for one. (3) For your last (or next) vendor or acquisition conversation, find and read the change-of-control clause. (4) Sketch a 100-day integration plan for a hypothetical acquisition of a team half your size — where does the stack merge, and on whose timeline?
