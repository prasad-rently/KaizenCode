🌱 The Gardener

A gardener can't yank a plant to make it grow. They pull the weeds, get the light and water right, and then get out of the way. Growing engineers is the same craft: you don't grow people, you create the conditions in which they grow themselves — and then resist the urge to do it for them.

Second module of the Leadership track. No runnable code — instead: a contrast to fix your 1:1s, an SBI feedback composer, a delegation ladder, decision scenarios to pressure-test your instincts, and a builder for this module's deliverable: your weekly 1:1 notes.

1The gardener's mindset

As an engineer, growth came from your effort on your problems. As a manager, your growth now happens through other people — and people don't respond to being compiled. They can't be debugged, refactored, or shipped. They can only be cultivated: given the right challenge, the right feedback, and the room to struggle productively.

This flips your instincts. The fastest way to solve today's problem is usually to do it yourself; the fastest way to build a team that solves problems is almost always the opposite. Every section below is a tool for trading a little short-term speed for a lot of long-term capability — the gardener's trade.

2The 1:1 — your highest-leverage half hour

The single most powerful tool you have is a recurring one-on-one. And the most common way new managers waste it is by turning it into a status update — something Slack, the board, or standup already cover. A status meeting is about the work. A real 1:1 is about the person.

❌ Status-update 1:1

"So, where are we on the API ticket?"
Project-by-project progress check
Manager talks 80% of the time
Cancelled the moment you're busy
They leave feeling audited

✅ Real 1:1

"What's on your mind this week?"
Their agenda first — it's their meeting
You listen 70%+; ask, don't tell
Protected — rarely cancelled, never by you
Career, growth, friction, feedback both ways

Good 1:1 questions open doors instead of checking boxes: "What's felt frustrating lately?" · "Where do you want to be in a year?" · "What am I doing that helps — or gets in the way?" · "What are you avoiding?" Then stay quiet long enough for a real answer. Let them cancel it if they're slammed; you cancelling it tells them they don't matter.

3Coaching vs directing — ask, don't tell

When someone brings you a problem, you have two modes. Directing = you give the answer. Coaching = you ask questions that help them find it. Directing is faster today; coaching builds judgment that compounds for years. Your default should drift toward coaching as people grow.

The hardest habit is staying curious one question longer instead of leaping to solve. Swap "here's what you should do" for:

  • "What have you already tried?"
  • "What do you think the options are?"
  • "What's the real challenge here for you?"
  • "If I weren't here, what would you do?"
Directing is still right sometimes: in a genuine emergency, on safety or non-negotiables, or with a true novice who has no map yet. The skill isn't "always coach" — it's choosing the mode on purpose instead of always reflexively telling.

4Feedback that lands — the SBI model

Most feedback fails because it's either too vague ("be more proactive") or an attack on character ("you're careless"). SBI fixes both by sticking to observable facts in three beats:

  • Situation — when and where, specifically. "In this morning's standup…"
  • Behavior — what they actually did, no interpretation. "…you cut Sam off twice before he finished."
  • Impact — the effect it had. "…he went quiet for the rest of the meeting, and I think we lost his read on the migration."

Notice what's absent: any claim about who they are. Facts are hard to argue with and easy to act on; labels ("you're rude") just trigger defensiveness. SBI works exactly the same way for praise — and specific praise is far more motivating than a generic "great job." Compose one now:

Situation — when & where
Behavior — what they did (just the facts)
Impact — the effect it had

5Delegation is a ladder, not a switch

New managers treat delegation as binary — either I do it, or I dump it on you. In reality it's a ladder of autonomy. The skill is matching the rung to how ready this person is for this task, and consciously climbing as they grow.

↑ most manager controlyou decide
1
Tell — you decide and announce it. Crises, non-negotiables, legal/safety.
2
Sell — you decide, but explain the why to win buy-in. Unpopular but necessary calls.
3
Consult — you gather their input, then you decide. Your call, informed by them.
4
Agree — you decide together, as equals. Shared ownership.
5
Advise — they decide; you offer your opinion first. They're driving, you're a passenger.
6
Inquire — they decide; you just ask to hear about it after. Trust, with a check-in.
7
Delegate — they own it end-to-end; you don't need the details. Full autonomy.
↓ most autonomythey decide

The two classic failures are opposite ends of the same mistake — not matching the rung to the person. Staying on rungs 1–2 with someone ready for 5–7 is micromanagement; jumping to 7 with someone who needs scaffolding is abdication dressed up as trust. Name the rung out loud ("this one's yours end-to-end" vs "check with me before you commit") so expectations are never a guess.

6What actually motivates engineers

Once pay is fair, more money barely moves engagement — and dangling bonuses can even reduce performance on creative work. Daniel Pink's Drive names the three intrinsic motivators that do work, and each maps to a tool you already have:

  • Autonomy — control over how, what, when, and with whom. This is what the delegation ladder buys. Prescribe the outcome, not the keystrokes.
  • Mastery — the pull to get visibly better at something that matters. Feed it with stretch assignments just past their current level, plus the coaching and feedback to actually learn from them.
  • Purpose — connection to a why bigger than the ticket. Relentlessly tie the work back to the user it helps or the goal it moves; never let people ship into a void.

A bored senior engineer is rarely a pay problem — they've run out of mastery or autonomy. The gardener's job is to notice the plant has outgrown its pot before it wilts.

8Deliverable — run four weekly 1:1s

Theory doesn't grow anyone; reps do. Your deliverable is to run (or realistically mock) four weekly 1:1s with someone, capturing notes each time — because the value of 1:1s compounds across weeks, when you can follow threads and hold people to their own goals. Use this to generate a clean, reusable note for one session, then repeat it weekly:

Report's name (optional)
Their agenda — what's on their mind (ask first, listen)
Wins & progress since last time
Growth focus — the one thing they're developing
Feedback for them (try SBI — specific, factual)
What they need from me / blockers to clear
Actions & owners for next week
Try it: (1) Work through all five scenarios — notice how often the growth answer means asking instead of telling. (2) Compose one real piece of SBI feedback you've been sitting on and actually deliver it this week. (3) For someone you manage (or a peer), place each task you'd hand them on the delegation ladder — are you a rung too low? (4) Run the 1:1 builder four weeks running; the notes are your deliverable.
