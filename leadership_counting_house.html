🧮 The Counting House

A CTO doesn't just spend the company's money — they're a steward of its capital, and often the owner of its single largest cost line. The leaders who get a real seat at the table are the ones who can read a P&L, defend a budget, and talk about margin, burn and unit economics as fluently as they talk about latency. You don't need an MBA; you need to stop being the person in the room who goes quiet when the numbers come up.

Third module of Stage 4 · Business Fluency. Two real calculators: a unit-economics (LTV:CAC) tool, and a 12-month engineering budget builder — the deliverable.

1Why a CTO speaks finance

Engineering is usually one of the two biggest expenses in a software company — often the biggest. That makes you a major steward of the company's capital, whether you think of yourself that way or not. When you can explain what your spend buys in financial terms — margin, capacity, risk reduction, growth — you stop being a cost center to be squeezed and become a partner in how the business is run. Financial fluency is the price of admission to the executive conversation.

2Reading a P&L — where engineering lives

The P&L (profit & loss / income statement) is the company's scoreboard. It flows top to bottom from what you earn to what you keep:

Revenue$10.0M
− COGS (cost to serve customers)$2.5M
= Gross profit (75% gross margin)$7.5M
− OpEx (R&D, sales, G&A)$6.0M
= Operating income$1.5M

The line that trips up engineers is COGS vs OpEx — and it matters, because COGS is subtracted before gross margin (the number investors obsess over), while OpEx sits below it:

COGS — cost to serve customers

Production cloud & infra (your bill!)
Storage & bandwidth for customers
Per-transaction third-party APIs
Customer support
→ hits GROSS MARGIN

OpEx — building & running the business

R&D / most engineering salaries
Dev & test environments
Sales, marketing, G&A, office
Internal tooling
→ below the gross-margin line

3Gross margin & COGS — your cloud bill lives here

Here's the fact every CTO should hold onto: your production cloud bill is COGS. Every dollar of infrastructure it takes to serve a customer comes straight out of gross margin — and gross margin is a huge driver of how the company is valued. A SaaS business at 80% gross margin is worth far more per dollar of revenue than one at 55%, because more of every sale drops through to profit.

That's why "just throw more servers at it" is a financial decision, not only a technical one, and why FinOps (cloud cost engineering) is now a CTO-level concern. Cutting cost-to-serve isn't penny-pinching — it directly lifts margin, and margin lifts valuation.

CapEx vs OpEx, briefly: a big one-time purchase (servers you own) is capital expenditure — it hits cash now but is spread across the P&L over years. A monthly cloud bill is operating expenditure — it hits the P&L as you go. Cloud turned most infra from CapEx into OpEx, which is why finance cares how it's classified.

4Unit economics & CAC / LTV

Zoom from the whole company down to a single customer: do they make money? Unit economics answers that, and two numbers dominate. CAC (customer acquisition cost) is what you spend to win one customer; LTV (lifetime value) is the gross profit that customer generates before they churn. The ratio LTV:CAC is the health of your growth engine — the rule of thumb is you want it above 3, and you want to recover CAC (the payback period) within roughly a year.

5Cash, burn & runway

The P&L shows profitability; the thing that actually kills companies is running out of cash. Two numbers you must always know: burn rate (net cash you spend per month) and runway = cash ÷ burn — how many months until zero. A startup with "8 months of runway" is telling you exactly how long you have to reach the next milestone or raise.

This directly shapes your decisions. Every hire adds to burn and shortens runway; a big infra commitment does too. When runway is short, the CTO's job is to spend deliberately — protect the critical roles and the work that reaches the next milestone, and defer the rest. "We can afford it over a year" is irrelevant if you run out of cash in eight months.

7Pressure-test your instincts

Finance for CTOs fails in predictable ways — treating the cloud bill as "not my concern," chasing growth with broken unit economics, cutting the wrong budget line, and ignoring runway. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run the unit-economics calculator with your product's real ARPU, margin, churn and CAC — is LTV:CAC above 3? (2) Build your 12-month budget and note what % is headcount; that's the line that actually moves the number. (3) Find out your company's current gross margin and runway — if you don't know them, that's today's homework. (4) Next time you propose infra spend, name its effect on gross margin, not just its dollar cost.
