👑 The Round Table

Somewhere on the way to CTO, the room changes. You stop being the most senior engineer explaining a decision upward, and become one peer among several — CEO, CFO, CPO, COO — each running their own function, each with their own scoreboard, all of you jointly accountable to a board that mostly can't read code. Technical brilliance stops being the differentiator; being a trusted, plain-spoken executive peer is.

Third module of Stage 5 · The CTO Craft. The centerpiece is a real board-deck builder — assemble an engineering update and one big ask into the one-page format a board actually reads.

1From tech lead to executive peer

As an engineering leader you were used to being the authority on technical matters, translating your team's work upward to people who deferred to your judgment on anything code-shaped. At the executive table that deference disappears — the CFO doesn't defer to you on architecture any more than you'd defer to them on hedging strategy, and neither of you defers to the other by default. You're all peers who must earn credibility with each other, continuously, through judgment other executives can see and trust — not through technical depth they can't evaluate.

The shift that trips up the most technically excellent leaders: stop translating engineering to the room and start representing engineering's interests in the room, in the room's native language — growth, risk, cash, customers. See The Storyteller for the mechanics of that translation; this module is about the room itself.

2Reading the other seats at the table

Each peer optimizes for something different, and none of it is malicious — it's their job. Knowing what each one is actually measured on turns friction into negotiation:

CEOThe whole story — growth, the narrative to the board and market, and the tie-breaker when functions disagree. Bring them choices with a recommendation, not raw problems; they're allocating scarce attention across every function, not just yours.
CFOCash, runway, margin, predictability. Engineering spend is the biggest controllable cost line they own no direct control over — so unpredictable burn or a surprise infra bill reads as a control failure on their desk. Forecast, and flag variance before they find it.
CPO/CRORoadmap velocity and revenue — "why can't we ship faster" is the default friction point, because they're measured on outcomes engineering delivers but doesn't fully control. See The Product Whisperer for the peer relationship in depth.
COO/GCOperations run smoothly, risk stays contained. First call when something breaks in production or a contract goes sideways — see The Fine Print and The Weathervane.

The skill isn't agreeing with every seat — it's disagree-and-commit: argue your position hard in the room with the full weight of your evidence, then once the CEO or the group decides, execute the decision as if it were your own idea, in public, without relitigating it in the hallway afterward. Executives who only commit to decisions they personally won lose the trust of every other peer within a quarter.

3What a board actually wants from engineering

Most engineering leaders over-prepare depth the board never asked for and under-prepare the three things they actually came for:

✅ What lands

Is the engine healthy? (2–3 numbers, trended)
What's the one big risk, and is it contained?
The one decision you need from them
Confidence conveyed by brevity + candor
Answers ready for the two hard questions

⚠️ What loses the room

A wall of dashboards with no narrative
Sprint-level detail no director can act on
Burying a real risk in "all green" optimism
No explicit ask — they leave unsure what you need
Getting defensive on the hard question

Board meetings run a predictable cadence — quarterly for most private companies, monthly board-committee touchpoints at scale — and engineering usually gets 10–15 minutes inside a packed agenda. Investor relations follows the same discipline in a higher-stakes room: in fundraising or diligence calls you are the credibility check on whether the technology can support the story the CEO is telling, so the same "health, risk, ask" shape — backed by evidence you can defend live — is what an investor is listening for too.

5When the role splits: CTO vs VP Engineering

In an early-stage company one person is both the technical visionary and the delivery manager. That stops scaling once engineering crosses roughly 40–60 people, or the company starts making bets — architecture, build-vs-buy, an AI strategy — that need dedicated external-facing attention a delivery calendar won't allow:

CTOOutward and forward-facing — technology strategy, architecture bets, board/investor technical credibility, M&A diligence, sometimes the public face of engineering (conference talks, analyst calls).
VP EngInward and delivery-facing — team structure, hiring, sprint execution, on-call, the day-to-day engineering org. Owns whether the roadmap actually ships.

The split only works with a genuinely clear line — usually strategy-and-external vs. org-and-delivery — and a CTO who resists it because "I want to still run the team" is usually the reason a growing company's technical strategy quietly stalls: nobody has the bandwidth to think two years out while also running today's stand-up. The Architect of Teams covers the org-design side of this split in depth.

6Pressure-test your instincts

The round table rewards candor and predictability, and punishes surprises and defensiveness. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Build the board deck above using your team's real numbers — does the "one big ask" survive being said out loud in 15 minutes? (2) Write down what each of your CEO, CFO and CPO is actually measured on this quarter — if you don't know, that's the gap to close first. (3) Find one recent decision you disagreed with and check whether you're still relitigating it in hallway conversations instead of having committed. (4) If your org is past ~50 engineers, sketch where a CTO/VP-Eng line would actually fall.
