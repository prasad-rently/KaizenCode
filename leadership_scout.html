🔭 The Scout

A scout rides out ahead of the team to find the right people and read the terrain. Hiring is the single most consequential — and least reversible — decision a manager makes: a great hire compounds for years, a bad one costs months of everyone's time and a chunk of the team's morale. Yet most people do it on gut feeling. This module is how to do it like a professional.

Third module of the Leadership track. No runnable code — instead: a structured-vs-vibes contrast, a scoring rubric, decision scenarios, and a builder for this module's deliverable: a full interview loop with rubrics for a backend role.

1Hiring is your highest-leverage decision

As an IC you multiplied your own effort. As a manager, who you hire quietly sets the ceiling on everything your team can ever do. A single great engineer can lift the whole group's bar; a single bad hire drains your time (managing them), your team's time (covering and correcting), and — worst of all — your credibility, because everyone can see it.

And it's nearly irreversible: hiring is easy, un-hiring is slow, painful, and demoralizing for everyone. That asymmetry is the whole reason to be systematic. The scout's discipline: a false positive (hiring the wrong person) costs far more than a false negative (passing on a good one). When you're genuinely torn, the answer is no.

2Sourcing — you can't hire who you never reach

Hiring starts long before the interview, at the top of the funnel. The best engineers usually aren't scrolling job boards — they're happily employed and have to be reached. Your pipeline comes from referrals (your team's networks are gold), targeted outreach, communities, and a reputation worth joining.

  • Referrals first, but widen deliberately. Referrals are high-signal — and they quietly clone your existing team. If you only hire from your people's networks, you inherit their blind spots. Diversity of any kind starts at sourcing: you cannot hire who you didn't put in the funnel.
  • The job description is a filter, not a wish list. A wall of 15 "required" technologies repels great generalists and attracts box-tickers. Write for the problem they'll own and the impact they'll have; list what's truly required separately from what's nice.
  • Candidate experience is your employer brand. Every rejected candidate talks. A fast, respectful, human process markets you to everyone they know; a ghosting black-hole does the opposite.

3Structured interviews beat vibes

The default interview — "let's just chat and see if there's a fit" — is close to worthless, and worse than worthless if you care about fairness. Unstructured interviews mostly measure how much the interviewer likes the candidate and how similar they are to them. That's not signal; that's bias with a job title.

❌ Unstructured "let's chat"

Different questions for each candidate
Measures likeability & similarity
"I just got a good feeling"
Can't compare candidates fairly
Bias rides in unchecked

✅ Structured & scored

Same core questions, every candidate
Scored against a defined rubric
"Evidence: here's what they did"
Apples-to-apples comparison
Bias has to get past the scorecard

A rubric is the fix: for each competency, decide in advance what strong, adequate, and weak answers actually look like, then score what you observed. Every interviewer gives one of four verdicts per competency:

Strong hire
Clear evidence they'd raise our bar on this competency. Rare and unambiguous.
Hire
Solid — meets the bar, with real evidence. The everyday "yes".
No hire
Gaps, or not enough signal to be confident. "Fine" lives here — and "fine" is a no.
Strong no
Clear misfit or a real red flag. One is enough to end it.
Don't average away a real gap. A firm "no hire" on a core competency should sink the hire even if every other score is glowing — a brilliant coder who can't collaborate is still a bad hire. The rubric exists so a single soft "they seemed nice" can't paper over a hard "they couldn't do the job."

4Design the loop — one competency per stage

The most common loop mistake is testing the same thing five times. If four of your five interviewers run a coding exercise, you've measured coding four times and everything else zero times — and you'll be blindsided later by the gaps you never checked.

Instead, give each stage one competency to own, so the loop as a whole covers the full picture with no overlap and no holes. A solid backend loop:

❌ Five interviews, one signal

Screen → coding
Round 1 → coding
Round 2 → coding
Round 3 → coding again
Debrief → "great coder, ship it"

✅ One competency per stage

Screen → motivation & basics
Coding → problem-solving & craft
System design → scale & tradeoffs
Behavioral → ownership & ambiguity
Values → collaboration, raises the team

Assign each interviewer their competency and the questions in advance, so nobody wings it and nobody doubles up. This is exactly what you'll design in the deliverable below.

5The debrief — decide on evidence, not impressions

The decision meeting is where good candidates get rejected and bad ones get hired, if you run it badly. The enemy is anchoring: the moment the most senior or most confident person says "strong yes," everyone else's judgment quietly bends to match. Structure it to keep every read independent:

  • Written scores first, discussion second. Each interviewer submits their score and the evidence for it before anyone speaks. No one sees the others' scores until they've committed their own.
  • Argue from evidence, not vibes. "I liked them" is not data. "They couldn't explain why they chose that datastore" is. Resolve disagreements by going back to what the candidate actually said and did.
  • Raise-the-bar, not no-red-flags. The question isn't "any reason not to hire?" — it's "is there real evidence this person makes the team better?" Absence of negatives is not presence of signal.
  • Torn means no. Because false positives cost more, a genuinely split room is a pass. "Let's take a chance" is how bad hires happen.

6Closing, comp & equity

A "yes" from you is only half the deal — the best candidates have options, and the offer stage is a two-way sell. You've been selling the whole time (that's what a great candidate experience is); now you close.

  • Move fast and stay human. Speed signals conviction, and great candidates go off the market quickly. A same-day "we'd love to have you," a personal call from the manager, and momentum beat a slow, formal process.
  • Sell to their real motivators. Back to autonomy, mastery, purpose (from The Gardener) plus growth, the team, and the mission. Money gets them in the room; those keep them.
  • Pay fairly against a band, not a negotiation. Decide the range from the role and level, not from how hard someone haggles. Lowballing a great hire to "win" the negotiation is pennies now, resentment later.
  • Be honest about equity. Most engineers don't really understand options, strike prices, vesting, or dilution — and a vague "it could be worth millions" pitch erodes trust the day they learn the reality. Explain it straight.
Internal pay equity is a landmine. Paying a hard-negotiating new hire 20% above a loyal teammate in the same role feels efficient — until it leaks (it always leaks), and your best existing engineer feels like a fool for staying. Hold the band, or move both.

7Onboarding — the hire isn't done at "yes"

You fought to find and close this person; the fastest way to waste that is a chaotic first month. A new hire's early experience sets their trajectory — and how loudly they'll rave or complain to the next candidate you try to source. Give every hire a plan:

  • First 30 days — land & learn. Environment set up day one, an onboarding buddy, a tour of the systems and the people, and a small, real, shippable task in week one so they get an early win and a taste of the workflow.
  • 60 days — contribute. Owning normal-sized work independently, in the flow of the team.
  • 90 days — own. Taking a meaningful slice end-to-end; you both have enough signal to confirm the hire was right.

Ramp expectations explicitly — nobody is fully productive in week two, and pretending otherwise just breeds impostor stress. Say out loud that month one is for learning.
