📍 The Mapmaker

A mapmaker doesn't invent the terrain — they show it honestly, mark the route, and are clear about what's charted and what's still fog. A roadmap is the same: not a promise of dated features, but a communication of intent and bets, and — just as importantly — of what you're deliberately not doing. The hardest, most valuable line on any roadmap is the cut line.

Second module of Stage 3 · Leading Delivery. This one is hands-on: a real in-browser RICE prioritizer that ranks your initiatives and draws the cut lines is the deliverable — a two-quarter roadmap built from evidence, not volume.

1A roadmap is a set of bets, not a list of dates

The most common roadmap is a Gantt chart of features with confident dates stretching four quarters out. It's comforting and almost entirely fiction — you don't know what you'll learn next month, let alone next quarter. A good roadmap communicates three honest things: what outcomes we're chasing, in what rough order, and how confident we are. It's a living statement of intent, not a contract.

The deeper shift is from outputs to outcomes. "Ship feature X" is an output; "cut checkout abandonment by 20%" is an outcome. Feature factories measure success by how much they build; the best teams measure it by what changes for the user. Roadmap the change you want, and stay flexible about the features that get you there.

❌ Feature-factory roadmap

A list of features with hard dates
Measures output ("we shipped 12 things")
Fixed 4 quarters out, pretends to certainty
Everything is committed; nothing is cut

✅ Outcome roadmap

Outcomes we're betting on, roughly ordered
Measures change ("abandonment −20%")
Confident near-term, fuzzy far-term
An explicit cut line — what we won't do

2OKRs — steer by outcomes

OKRs (Objectives & Key Results) are the tool for making outcomes concrete. An Objective is a qualitative, motivating direction ("make checkout effortless"); its Key Results are the measurable outcomes that prove you got there ("abandonment < 15%", "median checkout < 20s"). They align a team on why and let you measure success without dictating the features.

  • Key results are outcomes, not tasks. "Launch the new checkout" is a task (you either did it or didn't). "Abandonment under 15%" is a result (it says whether the work worked). If you can complete a KR without moving a metric, it's a task in disguise.
  • Few and ambitious. Three objectives with three KRs each, not thirty. OKRs are for focus; a long list is the opposite of focus.
  • Don't bolt them to comp. Tie bonuses to hitting OKRs and everyone sandbags the targets to be safe — Goodhart's Law again. Keep them a stretch, not a stick.

3Prioritize in the open — RICE & cost of delay

Every stakeholder's request is, to them, obviously the top priority. Without a shared method, prioritization defaults to politics and the HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion). Frameworks don't remove judgment — the inputs are estimates — but they drag the tradeoff into the open, where it can be discussed with evidence instead of decibels.

  • RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort. Reach = how many, per period; Impact = how much per person; Confidence = how sure you are (a humility multiplier); Effort = the cost. It rewards big, likely wins that are cheap to build.
  • WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size — do the work with the highest cost-of-delay per unit of effort first. This is why a two-week, high-urgency fix often beats a six-month epic, even if the epic is "bigger."
The framework informs; it doesn't decide. A RICE score is a conversation-starter, not an oracle — the confidence factor exists precisely because your numbers are guesses. Use it to structure the debate and expose assumptions, never to end thinking with a spreadsheet.

4Cost of delay — the most underused lever

Most teams prioritize by value or by effort. Far fewer ask the sharpest question: what does it cost us for every week we don't have this? That's cost of delay, and it reframes everything. A feature worth $1M that loses $0 by waiting a quarter is less urgent than a $200k fix bleeding $50k/week right now.

Cost of delay is what makes urgency legible. It's why "do the biggest project first" is usually wrong — you should do the highest cost-of-delay-per-effort first, ship value continuously, and keep the slow-bleeding items from festering while you polish something that could have waited.

5The art of saying no — and the cut line

Here's the executive truth: a roadmap is defined by what it leaves out. Saying yes is easy and popular; every yes without a corresponding no just quietly overcommits the team until nothing ships and everyone burns out. The skill that separates senior leaders is a graceful, visible no.

  • Make the tradeoff visible, don't hide it. The magic phrase is "yes, and here's what drops." You rarely have to refuse outright — you just show the cost. Faced with the actual thing that would be cut, most requesters reprioritize themselves.
  • Draw an explicit cut line. Rank the work, decide what capacity you truly have, and draw a literal line. Above it: committed. Below it: not this cycle — said out loud, not left as a vague "maybe later." An unstated no is how backlogs rot and stakeholders feel betrayed.
  • Protect focus like it's the scarce resource it is. Managing up on scope is mostly this: defending the team's ability to finish a few important things against the constant pressure to start many.

7Pressure-test your instincts

Roadmapping fails in predictable ways — false certainty, letting the loudest voice win, chasing size over urgency, and saying yes to everything. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run the prioritizer on your team's real backlog — then show a stakeholder the cut line, not just what's in. (2) For your top item, estimate its cost of delay per week; for your biggest item, do the same — the ranking may surprise you. (3) Rewrite one roadmap "feature" as an outcome with a metric. (4) Practice the sentence "yes, and here's what drops" until it's comfortable.
