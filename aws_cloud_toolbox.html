Compute — what actually runs your code

MUST $$

Your code needs a computer to run on — but you don't want to own one (buy it, plug it in, patch it, replace the fan at 2am). The cloud rents you one. The only real question: how much of the babysitting do you hand to AWS?

💡 Think of it like getting around a city. EC2 = buying a car (yours forever — you do the oil changes). Fargate = a taxi (you say where; they drive and maintain it). Lambda = a rental scooter (grab it for a 5-minute hop, pay only for the ride, it's gone after).
What actually is a “container”?
A lunchbox for your app. It seals your code and everything it needs to run — the right Node version, the libraries, the OS bits — into one box. That box runs identically on your laptop and in the cloud, so “works on my machine” finally means “works everywhere.”
Why not just one big server that never turns off?
For one tiny app, you can! It breaks when: (1) that server dies at 3am and your whole app is down, (2) traffic doubles and one box can't cope, (3) you deploy and users hit errors while it restarts. Fargate runs 2+ copies behind a load balancer, so none of those hurt.
How do I actually pick one?
Steady web app → Fargate. Tiny / spiky / occasional job → Lambda. Need a GPU or full control → EC2. Big team needing multi-cloud portability → EKS (Kubernetes). When unsure: Fargate.
💸 EC2's #1 surprise bill: it charges you 24/7 whether you have 1 user or zero. A t3.large left running all month is ~$60 for literally nothing. Rule one of cloud bills: turn off what you're not using — or use Fargate/Lambda, which scale toward zero on their own.
💸 Lambda flips the other way: dirt-cheap for bursts, but expensive if it runs constantly. Past a few hundred hours a month of steady traffic, plain Fargate costs less. Lambda is a scooter, not a daily commute.

Networking & the front door

MUST $

Your app needs two things that sound opposite: to be hidden (so nobody reaches your database directly) and to be reachable (so users hit your site over HTTPS). Three pieces make both true at once.

💡 Think of it like an office building. The VPC is the building with locked floors. The load balancer (ALB) is the reception desk out front — greets every visitor, checks them in, sends them to a free staffer. Route 53 (DNS) is the street address that gets people to the door. Your database sits on a locked upper floor with no public entrance.
Why can't I just put my database on the internet?
Because bots scan every public address on earth within minutes, trying default passwords. A database on the open internet is found and attacked almost immediately. Keeping it in a private subnet — reachable only by your app, never from outside — removes that entire class of disaster.
What does a load balancer actually do for me?
Three quiet miracles: it lets you run more than one copy of your app (so one crash isn't an outage), it deploys with zero downtime (new copies come up before old ones leave), and it handles HTTPS so your app code doesn't have to. It's the hinge that makes “highly available” possible.
💸 Watch the NAT Gateway. The networking piece nobody warns rookies about: a NAT Gateway (lets private servers reach out to the internet) costs ~$32/mo plus a fee per GB through it. On a quiet app it's often the biggest line on the bill. Real, but easy to forget you turned on.
Go deeper: the pieces & the independent option

The pieces: AWS VPC (the private network) + ALB (layer-7 HTTP front door) / NLB (layer-4, for raw TCP/UDP or static IPs) + Route 53 (DNS). Cross-cloud: GCP VPC + Cloud LB, Azure VNet + Load Balancer. Independent: Cloudflare — many teams put it in front of AWS for free DNS + DDoS + caching.

VerdictVPC + ALB + Route 53, all AWS-native, defined once in Terraform and then forgotten until a connectivity bug. Add an NLB only for non-HTTP protocols. Cloudflare in front is a popular, free extra layer.

CDN & edge delivery

REC $

If your only server is in Virginia, a user in Sydney waits for every byte to cross the planet — twice. A CDN keeps copies of your images and files in ~hundreds of cities, and hands each user the nearest copy.

💡 Think of it like a coffee chain. Without a CDN, there's one café and everyone flies to it. A CDN opens a branch in every city — you get your order from the one down the street, and the original kitchen isn't slammed.
What can a CDN cache — everything?
Anything that's the same for everyone: images, videos, CSS/JS, downloads, and “public” API responses. What it can't cache is per-user data (your feed, your inbox) — that still comes from your server. The trick is caching the 90% that's shared and letting the origin handle the personal 10%.
Why do people put Cloudflare in front of AWS?
Because its free tier is genuinely good and bundles three things at once — CDN, DNS, and a security layer (DDoS + firewall). You get global speed and a shield without adding to your AWS bill. It's so common it's basically a default pattern.
⚠️ The stale-cache trap. You ship a fix, but users still see the old file — because the CDN is happily serving the cached copy. Every CDN needs a “cache invalidation” (a purge) after you change a cached asset, or a versioned filename (logo.v2.png). Forgetting this is a rite of passage.
Go deeper: the contenders
OptionCharacter

Cloudflare (independent)~$30–40B-scale public co. (2025). Generous free tier; CDN + DNS + security in one.
Fastly / Akamai / bunny.netFastly = programmable edge; Akamai = the pioneer, enterprise scale; bunny = cheap.

VerdictCloudFront for one bill and tight AWS integration; Cloudflare for a free tier and a built-in security suite. Both are excellent — becomes a MUST the moment you have real images or global users.

Scaling

REC free

Traffic isn't a flat line — it's quiet at 3am and slammed at lunch. Auto Scaling adds app copies when it's busy and removes them when it's quiet, automatically, so you neither crash under load nor pay for idle machines.

💡 Think of it like a shop rostering staff. Auto Scaling watches the queue: crowd building? Call in more cashiers. Store empty? Send some home. You set the rule (“keep the line short”) once, and it staffs every rush and every lull for you.
What's wrong with just running lots of servers all the time?
You'd pay peak prices around the clock for capacity you use two hours a day — often 5–10× the bill. The opposite mistake (too few servers) means you crash the moment you get popular. Auto Scaling gives you enough, right now, and nothing more.
How does it know when to scale?
You pick a signal and a target — usually “keep average CPU around 60%.” When traffic pushes CPU above that, it adds a copy; when it drops, it removes one. You can also scale on memory, request count, or (with KEDA) queue depth.
⚠️ Scaling has a floor and a ceiling — set both. No max and a traffic spike (or a bug, or a bot) can scale you to a huge bill. No sensible min and a sudden rush finds you with too few copies to ramp fast enough. Always cap both ends.

VerdictTurn on AWS Auto Scaling on your Fargate service with a target-tracking policy (min 2, max 10, target CPU 60%). Set once; it pays for itself on every spike and every quiet night.

🧗 Trailmark needs… to survive Saturday mornings when everyone hits the trails and the app. → the policy ramps from 2 tasks to as many as 10 as load climbs, then scales back to 2 by Tuesday night. You wrote the rule once in the Compute step; here you just switch it on.

Relational database — the system of record

MUST $$

Every app has one thing it must never get wrong: what's true right now — who the user is, what they own, what they paid. That truth lives in a relational database, and you want AWS, not you, keeping it alive at 3am.

💡 Think of it like a meticulous ledger book. Every fact has its row, nothing contradicts anything else, and there's always a backup if the building burns down. Postgres is the ledger; RDS is the accountant AWS hires to keep it, back it up, and cover when it's sick.
What does “managed” actually save me?
The unglamorous, never-ending chores: automated backups, security patching, failover when a machine dies, and spinning up read replicas. You still design your tables and write queries — AWS just keeps the box healthy so you're not paged for it.
What's “Multi-AZ” and do I need it?
A hot standby copy of your database in a second data centre (Availability Zone). If the main one fails, AWS automatically flips to the standby in about a minute. For anything real: yes — it's the difference between a one-minute blip and an hours-long outage.
Postgres or MySQL?
Default to Postgres — richer features (JSON, full-text, extensions), and the most-loved database in developer surveys. MySQL is perfectly fine, but Postgres is the modern default you won't outgrow.
💸 A read replica is NOT a backup. It's a live copy for spreading out read traffic — so if you fat-finger a DELETE, the replica faithfully deletes too. Backups (automated snapshots) are your undo button; replicas are your speed boost. Real apps keep both.
Go deeper: the contenders
OptionCharacter
RDS / Aurora (AWS)Managed Postgres/MySQL; Aurora = AWS's faster, pricier re-engineering. Start plain RDS Postgres.
PostgreSQL (the engine)Open source, no owner, the most-loved DB in dev surveys. What you're actually running.
Neon / Supabase / PlanetScaleServerless-DB independents with lovely DX (git-like branching, scale-to-zero) — the hot challengers.

VerdictRDS Postgres, Multi-AZ for failover, a read replica when reads dominate. Aurora at scale; Neon/Supabase for side-projects and preview environments.

Object storage — everything that isn't a row

MUST $

Databases are for facts you query. But photos, videos, PDFs, backups? Stuffing those into a database is slow and pricey. Object storage is the giant, cheap, bottomless bucket for files.

💡 Think of it like an infinite coat-check. You hand over a file, get a ticket (its key/URL), and can retrieve it forever. It never fills up, and you pay pennies per “coat” per month.
Why not just store images in the database?
They bloat it, slow every backup, and cost far more per GB. The rule of thumb: the database stores the link to the file; S3 stores the actual file. Keep big blobs out of your rows.
What's a “presigned URL”?
A temporary, signed link that lets someone upload or download one specific file straight to/from S3 — without your server touching the bytes and without making the bucket public. It's the safe, standard way to handle user uploads.
What does “11 nines of durability” mean?
S3 keeps many copies of your object across separate machines and buildings, so the odds of ever losing one are 99.999999999%-against. Translation: your files are far safer there than on any disk you own.
💸 The two classic S3 traps: (1) a public bucket — the internet's favourite data leak; keep buckets private and serve via CloudFront or presigned URLs. (2) egress fees — downloading data out of AWS costs money, so serving lots of big files can surprise you (this is exactly R2's “zero egress” pitch).
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS S3 (2006; its API is a de-facto standard others clone) · GCS · Azure Blob · independents: Cloudflare R2 (zero egress fees), Backblaze B2, MinIO (self-hosted, S3-compatible). So influential that rivals describe themselves as “S3-compatible.”

VerdictS3 by default, private, with lifecycle rules aging cold data to Glacier. Look at R2 only if you serve big files and egress dominates the bill.

Cache — the hot path

REC $

Some answers are expensive to compute but asked for constantly. A cache keeps the answer in ultra-fast memory, so you compute it once and serve it a thousand times.

💡 Think of it like keeping milk in the fridge instead of driving to the farm for every cup of tea. The farm (your database) is the source of truth; the fridge (Redis) is the fast copy you actually reach for.
What should I cache first?
The slow, popular, rarely-changing stuff: a homepage feed, config, a user's profile. Don't cache data that's different on every request or changes every second — you'll spend more effort keeping it fresh than you save.
What's a TTL?
Time-to-live: how long a cached answer stays before it's thrown out and recomputed. Short TTL = fresher but more work; long TTL = faster but staler. You're literally dialling the trade between freshness and speed.
Why not just keep it in my app's memory?
That works with one server. The moment you run two (which you will, for reliability), each has its own memory and they disagree — one user sees new data, another sees old. Redis is one shared fridge that all your servers read from.
⚠️ Cache invalidation is famously one of the hard problems. The classic bug: you update the data but forget to clear the cache, so users keep seeing the old value. Before you cache anything, know exactly how that value gets refreshed or expires.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS ElastiCache (Redis/Valkey/Memcached) · Redis — a 2024 license split created the open Valkey fork (Linux Foundation; AWS backs it) vs Redis Ltd (~$2B private) · independents: Upstash (serverless, per-request), DragonflyDB.

VerdictElastiCache (Valkey/Redis) inside your VPC. Consider Upstash for serverless stacks where a per-request price beats a running node.

🧗 Trailmark needs… the feed to load fast without hammering Postgres. → cache the rendered feed per user in Redis with a 60-second TTL; also hold login sessions and the “5 uploads/min” rate-limit counter there.

Queues & events — the shock absorber

REC ¢

Some work is slow — resizing an image, sending an email, calling a flaky third party. Making the user wait for it is cruel. A queue lets you say “I'll handle that in a moment” and reply instantly.

💡 Think of it like a restaurant order rail. The waiter (your API) clips the order and goes back to serving; the kitchen (workers) cooks at its own pace. On a rush, orders pile on the rail — none get lost, and nobody waits at the counter.
Why not just do the slow work right away?
Because the user's request hangs until it finishes — and a burst of slow work times everyone out at once. Queue it, return fast, and let a pool of workers grind through the backlog steadily.
What's a “dead-letter queue”?
The tray for messages that keep failing (a corrupt photo, a bug). Instead of retrying forever and clogging the line, they're set aside for you to inspect later. A must-have safety valve — always configure one.
Queue vs SNS vs EventBridge?
SQS = one worker pool pulls a to-do list. SNS = shout one event to many listeners at once (fan-out). EventBridge = route events by rules and run scheduled jobs (cron). Different shapes of “something happened.”
⚠️ Assume every message can arrive twice. Queues guarantee “at least once” delivery, not “exactly once” — so make workers idempotent (processing the same message twice does no harm). Skip this and you get double-charges and double-emails.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS SQS (one of AWS's first services; planet-scale) + SNS + EventBridge · independents: Redis + BullMQ (simplest start), RabbitMQ (rich routing), GCP Pub/Sub. For a durable, replayable log, that's streaming — next block.

VerdictSQS for work queues (with a dead-letter queue), SNS/EventBridge for fan-out and scheduling. Redis+BullMQ only if you already run Redis and want the simplest thing.

🧗 Trailmark needs… to make thumbnails without stalling the upload. → on upload, drop a message on SQS; a Lambda worker pulls it, makes 3 sizes, writes them back to S3. The upload returns instantly; thumbnails appear a second later.

Streaming — a replayable log of everything

SIT $$

A queue is a to-do list: once a worker does a task, it's gone. A stream is a diary: every event is written down and kept, so many readers can go back and re-read the whole history.

💡 Think of it like a phone call vs a group chat. A call (queue) — say it, it's heard, it's over. A group chat (stream) — the message stays; new people scroll up, and everyone reads at their own pace.
Queue or stream — how do I tell?
If one worker should handle each item and then forget it → queue. If many systems need the same events, or you might replay history later → stream. Two readers of the same events is the giveaway you want a stream.
Why is this “situational”?
It's powerful and genuinely complex to run. Most apps thrive on plain queues for years. Reach for streaming only when you have a real firehose — analytics, event-sourcing, or several independent consumers.
💸 Kafka/MSK isn't cheap or hands-off — you pay for brokers running 24/7 whether or not events flow. Start with Kinesis (or even SQS) and adopt Kafka only when its ecosystem truly earns the ops cost.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: Apache Kafka (born at LinkedIn; the streaming standard) commercialised by Confluent (~$7–9B public, 2025) · AWS Kinesis (native, simpler) or MSK (managed Kafka) · Redpanda (Kafka-compatible, faster) · GCP Pub/Sub.

VerdictStart with Kinesis (least to run) on AWS; move to MSK/Confluent when Kafka's ecosystem (connectors, stream processing) earns its complexity.

NoSQL — key-value at any scale

SIT ¢

Relational databases are brilliant at complex questions across related tables. But some data is dead simple — “give me the value for this key” — just at enormous volume. That's where NoSQL shines.

💡 Think of it like a library vs a coat-check. Postgres is a library: rich, cross-referenced, great for “find all books by X that mention Y.” DynamoDB is a coat-check: hand over a ticket, get your item back in milliseconds — no browsing, just instant lookups, forever, at any scale.
When is NoSQL the wrong choice?
Whenever you'll want flexible, relational questions later. DynamoDB makes you design your queries up front; a brand-new access pattern can mean redesigning the whole table. If there's any doubt, start with Postgres.
Isn't Postgres JSONB “NoSQL enough”?
Very often, yes. Postgres can store schemaless JSON documents and index them — covering most “I need NoSQL” cases without adding a second database to run and learn. Reach for DynamoDB when scale or latency truly demands it.
⚠️ DynamoDB's trap is modelling, not cost. Design for the wrong access pattern and you're stuck with painful migrations or expensive full-table scans. Nail “how will I look this up?” before you create the table.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS DynamoDB (powers Amazon.com's cart at Prime-Day scale; the 2007 Dynamo paper seeded a generation of NoSQL) · independents: MongoDB Atlas (document-shaped), Cassandra/ScyllaDB (self-managed scale) · often the honest answer is just Postgres JSONB.

VerdictReach for DynamoDB in Lambda-native stacks or a table with brutal scale + simple keys. Otherwise, try Postgres JSONB first.

Search — what LIKE '%…%' can't do

SIT $$

Users expect Google-quality search: typos forgiven, best matches first, instant. Your database's LIKE '%word%' can't do that — it's slow and clueless about relevance. A search engine is built for exactly this.

💡 Think of it like the index at the back of a book vs flipping every page. LIKE reads every page every time; a search engine builds the index once, then jumps straight to the best matches — even if you misspelled the word.
Do I need a search engine on day one?
Usually no. Postgres full-text search is free and surprisingly capable. Add a dedicated engine only when search becomes a feature users actively judge you on and rank-quality matters.
OpenSearch or Algolia?
OpenSearch = powerful, AWS-native, more to run and tune yourself. Algolia = the fastest path to a great search UX, as a paid SaaS. You're choosing control vs convenience.
⚠️ A search index is a copy, not the source of truth. You must keep it in sync — when a trail changes, re-index it. Forgetting is why search sometimes returns results that no longer exist.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS OpenSearch (a 2021 fork of Elasticsearch after a license dispute with Elastic NV, ~$8–12B public) · independents: Algolia (best DX, $$), Meilisearch / Typesense (light, self-hostable) · and Postgres full-text search goes surprisingly far for free.

VerdictPostgres FTS until it strains, then OpenSearch (AWS-native) or Algolia (fastest to great UX, at a price).

Analytics & warehouse — questions, not transactions

SIT $$

Your production database serves one user's request in milliseconds. Ask it “sum every order across three years” and it grinds — slowing every real user while it does. Big analytics belongs somewhere separate.

💡 Think of it like a shop floor vs a back office. The shop floor (your app DB) serves customers fast. The back office (a warehouse) crunches the month's numbers — you don't do the accounting at the checkout counter mid-rush.
Do I need Snowflake on day one?
Almost never. A read replica + a tool like Metabase answers most early analytics without a warehouse at all. Graduate when reports get big, frequent, or start competing with live traffic.
Redshift vs Athena — what's the difference?
Redshift is a warehouse you load data into and keep running. Athena runs SQL directly over raw files in S3, pay-per-query, nothing to keep running — the cheaper, lazier way to start.
⚠️ Never run analytics on your production database. The #1 way a “quick report” takes the whole app down is an analyst's giant query locking tables at lunchtime. Point analytics at a replica or a warehouse — never the primary.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS Redshift (warehouse), Athena (SQL over S3 files), Glue (ETL) · the independents dominate: Snowflake (~$50B public, 2025), Google BigQuery, Databricks (~$60B+ private, the lakehouse) · small scale: DuckDB over S3, or a read replica + Metabase.

VerdictHonest first step: a read replica + Metabase. Grow into Athena over S3, or Snowflake/BigQuery when analytics becomes a real function.

Source & CI/CD — the release robot

MUST ¢

Copying files onto a server by hand is how mistakes ship at 5pm on a Friday. CI/CD is a robot that takes every merge and does the whole boring, error-prone release for you — the same way, every time.

💡 Think of it like a dishwasher. You could wash each plate by hand (and occasionally drop one), or load the rack, press start, and get a clean, consistent result every cycle. Your pipeline is the dishwasher for shipping code.
What's the difference between CI and CD?
CI (Continuous Integration) = on every push, automatically build and test, so broken code is caught in minutes. CD (Continuous Delivery/Deployment) = automatically ship what passed. Together: merge → tested → live, hands-free.
What actually goes in the pipeline?
Usually a checklist a robot runs: install dependencies → run tests → build the Docker image → push it to the registry → tell your compute to roll out the new version. You write it once as YAML; it runs on every push forever.
Why is “the merge button my deploy button”?
Because the pipeline triggers on merge. Releasing becomes something anyone can do safely, with a full audit trail (who merged what, when) — instead of a scary manual ritual only one person remembers how to perform.
⚠️ A green pipeline is only as good as its tests. With no tests, CI/CD will faithfully, automatically ship your bugs to production faster than ever. Add real tests, or you've just automated the mistake.

Build artifacts — the image & its shelf

MUST ¢

You built and tested your app on your laptop. How does the cloud run the exact same thing — same code, same libraries, same everything? You seal it into a Docker image, and store that image where your servers can grab it (a registry).

💡 Think of it like a shipping container. Before containers, loading a ship was chaos — every cargo a different shape. Docker did for software what the steel box did for freight: one standard box any crane (any cloud) can lift, unchanged.
Image vs container — what's the difference?
An image is the frozen recipe (your app, packaged up). A container is a running copy of it. Build the image once, run many containers from it — like a class and its instances.
Why a registry — can't I just build on the server?
You could, but then every server builds slightly differently and clean rollbacks get hard. Build once in CI, push the exact image to a registry, and every server pulls that identical, versioned artifact.
What's a “tag”?
The version label on an image, like trailmark-api:sha-abc123. Tagging by git commit means you always know exactly which code is running — and can redeploy an old tag to roll back in seconds.
💸 Registries charge for storage, and CI pushes a new image every commit — hundreds a month. Add a lifecycle rule to auto-delete old untagged images, or you'll slowly pay to store thousands of builds nobody will ever run again.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: Docker (the image format is now the open OCI standard, so it outlives any vendor); alternatives Podman, Buildpacks. Registry: AWS ECR (in-region, IAM-native, no egress) vs Docker Hub / GitHub Container Registry.

VerdictDocker to build, ECR to store — same region as your compute so pulls are fast and free. Add a lifecycle rule to prune old tags.

🔐

Identity & access (IAM)

MUST free

Every action in AWS — read a file, start a server, delete a database — is an API call, and IAM checks “are you allowed to do this?” before each one. Get it right and a stolen key is nearly useless; get it wrong and one leak owns everything.

💡 Think of it like keycards in an office. A new hire's card opens their floor and the kitchen — not the CEO's office or the server room. IAM gives every user and every app a keycard scoped to exactly what it needs, and nothing more.
What's “least privilege,” and why does everyone repeat it?
Give each thing permission to do only what it actually needs. So when (not if) a credential leaks, the damage is contained to that tiny scope instead of your whole account. It's the single highest-leverage security habit there is.
Roles vs users vs keys — what do I give my app?
A role. A user has long-lived keys that can leak; a role hands your app short-lived, auto-rotating credentials with no secret to steal. Humans log in via SSO; apps assume roles.
Why separate accounts for dev and prod?
So a mistake in dev physically cannot touch prod — different account, different blast radius. It's the strongest isolation AWS offers, and it's free.
⚠️ The lazy trap: attaching AdministratorAccess or a "*" policy “just to make it work.” It works — and now anything that touches that role can do anything in your account. Start narrow; add a permission only when something's actually denied.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS IAM is intrinsic — no alternative (GCP IAM / Azure RBAC are its siblings). The identity provider in front is often Okta (~$15B public) or Microsoft Entra. At scale, IAM Identity Center + Organizations give one login across many accounts.

VerdictWrite least-privilege policies; give apps roles, not keys. Adopt Identity Center + account-per-environment once you're past a toy. Solo learners: one account + one IAM user is fine.

Secrets & keys

MUST ¢

Your app needs the database password and a few API keys to run. Where do those live? Never in your code or git — bots scan public repos for leaked keys within seconds. A secrets store keeps them safe and hands them to your app at startup.

💡 Think of it like a hotel safe. You don't tape your passport to the door (a password in your code); you lock it in the safe, and only you — with the right key — can open it. Secrets Manager is the safe; your app's IAM role is the key.
What's actually wrong with a .env file or hardcoding?
It ends up in git history, backups, Slack pastes, and CI logs — and once a secret is in git, it's effectively public forever, even if you delete it later. A secrets store keeps the value in exactly one guarded place.
Secrets Manager or Parameter Store?
Parameter Store is free and covers ~90% of needs — start there. Upgrade to Secrets Manager when you want automatic rotation (it can change a DB password on a schedule). Both are fine choices.
What is KMS doing in the background?
KMS holds the encryption keys that scramble your data “at rest” (in S3, RDS, disks). You rarely touch it directly — you tick “encrypt,” and KMS makes “is your data encrypted?” answer yes.
⚠️ Deleting a leaked secret from git doesn't un-leak it — it's in the history, and anyone who cloned the repo already has it. If a key ever hits git, rotate it (issue a new one, revoke the old) immediately. Prevention with a secrets store is far cheaper than that cleanup.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS Secrets Manager (auto-rotation) / SSM Parameter Store (free 90% version) + KMS · independents: HashiCorp Vault (→ IBM ~$6.4B, 2024; powerful, heavy), Doppler / Infisical (SaaS DX).

VerdictParameter Store to start, Secrets Manager when you need rotation; KMS ticked on every store so “encrypted at rest?” is always yes. A .env on a server is what these replace.

End-user auth — sign-up & login

SIT ¢ · MUST if you have user accounts

What's a JWT, and why does it matter?

Audit, threat & compliance

MUST ¢

Three jobs in one: know what happened (for the day something goes wrong), catch attackers early, and prove to auditors you're safe. AWS gives you cheap, always-on tools for each — the trick is switching the recorder on before the incident, not after.

💡 Think of it like a building's security kit. CloudTrail is the CCTV that records everyone in and out. GuardDuty is the alarm that notices a break-in. WAF is the bouncer turning away troublemakers at the door. You install all three before the burglary.
Which one do I turn on first?
CloudTrail. It records every action in your account, it's nearly free, and after any incident it's the only thing that can tell you who did what. On before you think you need it.
What does a WAF actually block?
Common web attacks: SQL-injection attempts, known-bad bots, and floods of requests from one IP (rate limiting). It sits in front of your app and filters the obvious malicious traffic before it lands.
When do I care about Config / Inspector / Macie?
When a customer or auditor asks for SOC 2 / ISO. They continuously check “any public buckets? MFA everywhere? any exposed PII? any known CVEs?” — the exact evidence auditors want.
⚠️ Turn CloudTrail on now, not after the breach. It only records what happens after it's enabled — so flipping it on during an incident means you've already lost the history you need. It's near-free; there's no reason it isn't already on.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: mostly AWS-native and cheap-for-coverage (CloudTrail, GuardDuty, WAF+Shield, Config/Inspector/Macie, Dependabot+Trivy). Logs, metrics & alarms

MUST $

When your app misbehaves at 2am you have exactly two questions: “is something wrong?” and “what?” Metrics, logs and alarms answer both — they're the dashboard and the smoke detector for your running app.

💡 Think of it like a car's dashboard. Metrics are the gauges (speed, fuel, temperature), logs are the detailed trip record, and alarms are the red warning light that says “pull over now.” Driving without them is driving blindfolded.
Metrics vs logs — what's the difference?

What should I actually alarm on?
User-facing pain, not internals. “Error rate above X” or “p95 latency above Y” means users are hurting. Alarming on every CPU wiggle just trains you to ignore alerts — the dreaded alarm fatigue.
Why do teams leave CloudWatch for Datadog?

💸 Observability bills sneak up on you. Verbose logging and high-cardinality metrics can cost more than the servers you're watching — “Datadog bill rivalling the compute bill” is a running industry joke. Log what you'll actually read; sample the rest.

Tracing — where did the milliseconds go?

SIT $

When your app is one service, “why is this slow?” is easy. When it's five services calling each other, the slow bit hides in the hand-offs between them. Tracing follows a single request across every hop and shows exactly where the time went.

💡 Think of it like tracking a parcel. “It's late” isn't useful; the tracking page showing it sat in one depot for two days is. A trace is the tracking page for one request across your services.
How is tracing different from logs and metrics?
Metrics say “requests are slow”; logs show one service's view; a trace stitches together the whole journey of one request end-to-end — so you can see it spent 1.8s waiting on the search service and 50ms everywhere else.
Why is this “situational”?
With one or two services, logs answer “which is slow?” fine. Tracing earns its setup once you have enough services that the answer isn't obvious — usually past ~3 talking to each other.
⚠️ Instrument with OpenTelemetry, not a vendor's SDK. OTel is the open standard, so you can switch tracing backends (X-Ray, Jaeger, Honeycomb, Datadog) later without re-instrumenting every service. Lock-in here is painful and avoidable.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: OpenTelemetry is the CNCF vendor-neutral standard — instrument once, send anywhere (X-Ray, Jaeger, Tempo, Honeycomb, Datadog APM). Honeycomb pioneered high-cardinality debugging.

VerdictStandardise on OpenTelemetry regardless of backend — it keeps you portable. Adopt when you have more than ~3 services and “which hop?” stops being answerable from logs.

GenAI models

SIT $

Want your app to summarise, chat, caption a photo, or pull data out of messy text? You don't train anything — you send a prompt to a large language model over the internet and get an answer back. The only real choice: call it through AWS, or call the model-maker directly.

AI building blocks

SIT ¢

Some AI jobs are so common they're already solved and sold by the call: read text off an image (OCR), spot what's in a photo, turn speech into text. No training, no model — just one API call and a result.

💡 Think of it like kitchen appliances vs cooking from scratch. You don't build a blender to make a smoothie — you press a button. Rekognition, Textract and Transcribe are single-button appliances for the most common AI tasks.
When do I use these vs a big LLM?
Use a task API when you need one specific, well-defined job done cheaply and reliably (OCR a receipt, transcribe a call). Reach for a multimodal LLM when you'd otherwise chain several of them, or you need reasoning about the content, not just extraction.
Are they accurate enough?
For mainstream cases, yes — they're trained on huge datasets. For niche or high-stakes accuracy (medical, legal), test carefully and expect to review outputs. “Good enough” is task-dependent.
💸 These charge per image / page / minute, and a bulk backfill (re-processing your whole photo library at once) can spike the bill. Batch deliberately and, again, keep a Budget alert on.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS Textract (documents → text), Rekognition (image labels/faces), Transcribe (speech → text) · mirrored by Google Document AI/Vision/Speech and Azure Cognitive Services · increasingly one multimodal LLM does several at once (or open-source Tesseract/Whisper).

VerdictReach for a task API for one common job done cheaply; consider a multimodal LLM when you'd otherwise chain several of them.

🧗 Trailmark needs… to auto-tag what's in a photo (waterfall, summit, forest) for search. → Rekognition labels each upload; the labels feed the OpenSearch index so “photos with waterfalls” just works.

Custom ML

SIT $$$

Everything above uses someone else's AI. This is for when you build your own model — a recommender, a fraud detector, a demand forecast — trained on your data. It needs a whole platform to train, host and monitor, and a team who knows how.

💡 Think of it like takeout vs running a restaurant kitchen. Calling an LLM is takeout — fast, no staff. SageMaker is the professional kitchen: powerful and bespoke, but you need chefs (an ML team) to run it.
How do I know I need this and not just an LLM?
If your problem is “understand/generate language or images,” a hosted LLM or task API is almost always faster and cheaper. You need custom ML only when the model must learn from your specific data — your users' behaviour, your fraud patterns — something no general model has seen.
What's the honest first step before SageMaker?
A simple heuristic in your normal code. “Most popular this week,” “people you follow also liked” — plain SQL gets you ~80% of a recommender with none of the ML overhead. Reach for SageMaker only when that ceiling is real.
💸 Custom ML is the most expensive corner of this page. GPU training runs and always-on inference endpoints add up fast — and an idle endpoint bills 24/7 like any server. Don't stand one up until a simpler approach has clearly hit its limit.
Go deeper: the contenders

Contenders: AWS SageMaker (notebooks, training, registry, endpoints) · independents: Databricks (~$60B+ valuation, the lakehouse leader), Google Vertex AI, Hugging Face, Modal/Replicate (serverless GPU).

VerdictOverkill unless you have an ML team. Then SageMaker for AWS-native pipelines, or Databricks if your data + ML live together in a lakehouse.
