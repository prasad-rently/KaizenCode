📖 The Storyteller

At senior levels, your impact is capped by your ability to make other people act on your ideas — and that runs almost entirely on communication. The best technical plan, badly explained, loses every time to a worse plan told well. Clear writing and clear speaking aren't soft skills bolted onto the job; at this altitude, they largely are the job.

Second module of Stage 4 · Business Fluency. Hands-on: a real one-page CFO memo builder is the deliverable — pitch a technical investment in language a finance chief will act on.

1Communication is the multiplier

As an IC, your work spoke for itself — the code either ran or it didn't. As a leader, your work is other people's work, and that only happens if you can transfer an idea from your head into theirs clearly enough that they move. A brilliant strategy nobody understands has zero impact; a mediocre one everyone rallies behind ships. Communication is the lever that converts your thinking into organizational action.

The good news: it's a learnable craft, not a talent. It comes down to a few repeatable moves — lead with the answer, translate into the reader's language, wrap data in a story, and ruthlessly cut. This module is those moves.

2The pyramid principle — answer first

Engineers are trained to reason bottom-up: here's the context, here's the analysis, and therefore — at the end — here's the conclusion. It's how you derive an answer, and it's exactly backwards for communicating one. Busy readers (Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle) want the answer first, then the supporting reasons, then the detail beneath those — so they can stop reading the moment they're convinced.

❌ Bottom-up (how you derived it)

Background and context first
Then the analysis…
The ask buried in paragraph 5
Reader waits for the point
They stop reading before the ask

✅ Pyramid (answer-first)

The recommendation in line one
Then the 3 reasons it's right
Detail sits beneath each reason
Skimmable top-down
Approve — or ask one question — in 30s

This is the written cousin of BLUF (bottom line up front) from The Weathervane. Put the conclusion in the first sentence and structure everything else as support for it. If a reader gets only your first line, they should still get your point.

3Speak their language — translate to money & risk

The single most common reason engineers fail to get buy-in: they pitch in engineering currency (elegance, tech debt, latency) to people who bank in business currency (money, risk, time, customers). Nobody funds "refactoring"; they fund "reclaiming a third of our engineering capacity." Same thing — one is translated. Run every point through the "so what?" test until it lands in something the listener already cares about.

❌ What engineers say

"We need to pay down tech debt."
"Improve CI/CD and observability."
"Migrate to microservices."
"Reduce p99 latency."

✅ What execs act on

"~30% of our time goes to working around old code — this reclaims it."
"Ship faster, with fewer customer-facing outages."
"Let teams release independently, so we move faster."
"The app feels instant, so fewer customers give up and leave."

4Storytelling with data

Data doesn't persuade on its own — a wall of twelve charts overwhelms and convinces no one. Humans are wired for narrative, so the move is to wrap the numbers in a simple story arc: setup (here's the situation) → tension (here's the problem, and what it's costing) → resolution (here's the fix, and the result). The data becomes the evidence inside the story, not a substitute for it.

  • One chart, one message. Each visual should make exactly one point, and you should be able to say that point in a sentence. If a chart needs a paragraph to explain, it's doing too much.
  • Lead with the "so what," not the axes. "Incidents doubled after the last launch" beats "here is a time-series of incident counts." Tell them what it means, then show the chart as proof.
  • Round and simplify. "About a third" sticks; "31.7%" evaporates. Precision is for the appendix; memorability is for the room.

5The one-page memo & presenting to execs

Amazon famously banned slide decks for big decisions in favor of narrative memos — because prose forces the logic slides let you hide. You can't paper over a hole in your argument with a bullet point and a nice transition; you have to write the actual sentence, and if it doesn't hold up, you see it. Writing the memo is the thinking.

  • Lead with the ask. First line: what you want and why it matters. Exec-summary and board comms follow the same rule — the decision up front, the support below.
  • Anticipate the hard question and pre-answer it. A CFO will ask "what's the return, and what if it fails?" Put the ROI and the downside-with-mitigation in the memo, before they have to ask. Anticipating the objection is what makes you look ready.
  • Be ruthlessly short. One page beats ten. Brevity isn't laziness — it's respect for the reader's time and proof you know what actually matters. Cutting is the last 50% of the work.

6Deliverable — the one-page CFO memo

Pitch a real technical investment to a CFO in one page. Fill in the pieces (or take the sample) and the builder assembles them into a pyramid-structured, money-framed memo — the ask up front, the numbers, the cost of inaction:

The investment — what you want to do (plain language)
Situation — the current state, in business terms
What's changing / getting worse (the tension)
The cost — people / time / $
The return — in the CFO's currency (money / risk / time)
Cost of doing nothing (the risk)
The specific ask — the decision you need

7Pressure-test your instincts

Communication fails in predictable ways — burying the ask, pitching in jargon, drowning the point in data, and hoping the hard question won't come. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Take your last long update and rewrite it so the ask is the very first sentence. (2) Generate the CFO memo, then read only line one — does it stand alone? (3) Run your next technical pitch through the "so what?" test until every point lands in money, risk, time, or customers. (4) Before your next big proposal, write the one-pager before the deck — let the prose expose the gaps.
