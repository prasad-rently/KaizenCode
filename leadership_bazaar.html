🏪 The Bazaar

Every product lives in a marketplace, and how it makes money shapes everything about it — including what you build. A CTO who treats the business model as "someone else's department" ends up building the wrong things beautifully. Understand how the company captures value — its metrics, its pricing, its sales motion — and you become a partner in strategy, not just its executor.

Fourth module of Stage 4 · Business Fluency. Two real tools: a SaaS-metrics calculator (NRR, magic number, rule of 40) and a pricing-teardown builder — the deliverable.

1Pricing is product

It's tempting to think pricing is a spreadsheet the finance or sales team owns. In reality, how you charge is a product decision, and it feeds straight back into engineering. Charge per seat and you'll build admin and user-management features; charge by usage and you'll invest in metering, quotas, and cost-to-serve; go freemium and your architecture has to make the free tier cheap to run at huge scale. The business model quietly writes half your roadmap — so you'd better understand it.

2The SaaS metrics that matter

These extend the P&L literacy from The Counting House into the language of a subscription business. The one to tattoo on your brain is NRR.

  • ARR / MRR — annual (or monthly) recurring revenue: the predictable subscription run-rate. The heartbeat of a SaaS company.
  • NRR (net revenue retention) — of the revenue you had a year ago, how much do those same customers pay you now, after expansion and churn? Above 100% means you grow even if you never sign a new customer — the holy grail. 120%+ is world-class.
  • Churn — the leak. Revenue churn (dollars lost) matters more than logo churn (customers lost); losing one whale hurts more than ten minnows.
  • Magic number — net new ARR ÷ prior sales & marketing spend. Sales efficiency: above ~1 means every $1 of S&M buys more than $1 of new recurring revenue — pour it on.
  • Rule of 40 — growth rate % + profit margin % should exceed 40. It captures the growth-vs-profitability trade the whole company is judged on.

3Value-based pricing & packaging

The most common pricing mistake engineers make is cost-plus: "it cost us $X to build, so charge $X plus margin." That leaves enormous money on the table and anchors on the wrong thing. Price to the value the customer receives, not what it cost you to make — a feature that saves a customer $100k/year can command far more than its build cost, and one that saves nothing can't be rescued by any markup.

Packaging — how you bundle features into tiers — is often more powerful than the price itself. Good-better-best tiers let you serve a hobbyist and an enterprise with the same product, and steer buyers toward the middle. The features you gate behind the higher tiers decide who buys and how they expand.

4The three pricing models

Most SaaS pricing is a variation on three shapes. The key question for each: does the thing you charge for — the value metric — scale with the value the customer actually gets?

Per-seat

Pay per user.

+ Simple, predictable.

− Breaks when value ≠ headcount; users share logins.

Usage-based

Pay for what you consume.

+ Tracks value; low entry; expands automatically.

− Unpredictable bills; needs metering.

Freemium

Free tier → paid upgrade.

+ Viral adoption, land-and-expand.

− Free users cost you; low convert rates.

The winning pattern is usually land and expand: get in cheap (free tier or a small seat count), deliver value, and let the account grow — which is exactly what drives NRR above 100%. Align the value metric well and pricing becomes a growth engine; align it badly and heavy users feel gouged while light users churn.

5How enterprise sales works — and engineering's role

Selling to a large company is a different sport from a self-serve signup. It's a months-long, multi-threaded motion: a rep (AE) runs the deal, a sales engineer (SE) handles the technical evaluation, and it passes through a proof-of-concept, a security review, procurement, and legal before a signature. Deals are large and lumpy, and a single champion inside the account often makes or breaks them.

Here's what surprises new leaders: a lot of this lands on engineering, and it's real go-to-market work, not a distraction.

  • Security & reliability are sales assets. That 300-question security questionnaire, the SOC 2 report, the uptime SLA — they directly unlock (or kill) enterprise deals. Investing in them is investing in revenue.
  • SEs and POCs need engineering air-cover. Supporting the technical sale — a smooth POC, a fast answer to a hard architecture question — is often the difference in a big deal.
The feature-for-a-deal trap. Sales will want to promise a custom feature to close a whale. Sometimes right — but if every big deal bends the roadmap, you end up with a Frankenstein product built for accounts, not a market. The test: is it generalizable to other customers? If yes, maybe. If it's a true one-off, push for a paid services engagement or hold the line — one deal shouldn't own your product.
