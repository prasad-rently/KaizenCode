📜 The Fine Print

Somewhere on the way to CTO, the documents you're responsible for stop being pull requests and start being contracts. You'll sign or approve agreements worth millions, and you own the legal risk hiding in your own dependency tree — a single wrong license or an unsigned IP assignment can sink an acquisition or trigger a lawsuit. You don't become a lawyer. You become the person who can spot the landmine, and who knows exactly when to call one.

Second module of Stage 5 · The CTO Craft. The centerpiece is a real dependency-license auditor — paste your dependencies and see which licenses are safe and which are ticking.

1Why a CTO reads the fine print

Legal risk is one of the few things that can end a company overnight — a copyleft violation that forces you to open-source your product, a data-processing breach that draws a regulator, a contractor who never assigned their IP and now owns a chunk of your codebase. None of these are visible in a demo, and all of them land on the CTO's desk. Your job isn't to draft the contracts; it's to read them well enough to know which clauses matter, spot the risks engineers create, and pull in counsel before — not after — you sign.

2The contracts you'll meet

A handful of acronyms cover most of what crosses your desk. Know what each is for, and read the parts that bind engineering:

MSAMaster Service Agreement — the master terms for the whole relationship (liability, IP, termination). Signed once; everything else hangs off it.
SOWStatement of Work — the specific scope, deliverables and price for one engagement under an MSA.
SLAService Level Agreement — your uptime/performance commitments and the service credits (penalties) if you miss. Engineering has to actually deliver these.
DPAData Processing Agreement — how personal data is handled (GDPR/CCPA). Required for almost any B2B deal touching user data.
NDANon-Disclosure Agreement — confidentiality. The low-stakes one you'll sign the most.
The SLA is a promise engineering keeps, not sales. A "99.99% uptime" clause is ~53 minutes of allowed downtime per year; 99.9% is ~8.8 hours. Sales will happily promise numbers your systems can't hit. Never let an SLA be signed that engineering hasn't sanity-checked against real reliability and the penalty math.

3Open-source licensing & your dependency tree

Every dependency you pull in comes with a license — a legal contract governing how you may use it — and those obligations flow all the way down your transitive tree, including packages nobody chose directly. The critical split is permissive vs copyleft:

✅ Permissive (MIT, Apache, BSD, ISC)

Use freely, even in closed-source
Just preserve attribution / NOTICE
No duty to share your own code
The safe default — most of npm/PyPI
Apache adds a patent grant (a plus)

⚠️ Copyleft (GPL, AGPL, LGPL)

Sharing your source may be required
GPL: distribute your app → open-source it
AGPL: even a network service triggers it
LGPL/MPL: lighter — dynamic linking is OK
Great to use as a tool, risky as a dependency

The one that catches SaaS companies is AGPL: normal GPL copyleft only triggers when you distribute software, but AGPL extends it to offering the software over a network — so embedding an AGPL library in your web service can, in principle, obligate you to release your service's source. That's usually a hard no for a proprietary product without legal sign-off or a commercial license. Audit for it below.

5IP assignment, vendors & cyber insurance

Three more places the fine print bites:

  • IP & invention assignment — does the company actually own its code? Every employee and, crucially, every contractor must sign an IP-assignment agreement. Contractor work is not automatically "work for hire" — without an explicit assignment, the contractor may own what they built. An unassigned chunk of core code is a classic diligence deal-killer; find and fix these before an acquisition, not during.
  • Procurement & vendor negotiation. This is the buy-side of The Bazaar. Redline the MSA; watch for auto-renewals, uncapped price ratchets, and lock-in that makes leaving painful. Push for a security addendum and DPA. Cap your own liability. The vendor's standard contract is written for the vendor — negotiate it.
  • Cyber insurance. Covers the costs of a breach or ransomware — forensics, notification, legal, sometimes the ransom. Increasingly required by enterprise customers and boards. It's a backstop, not a substitute for security (see risk management); insurers now demand real controls (MFA, backups) before they'll even cover you.

6Pressure-test your instincts

The fine print bites in predictable ways — viral licenses, unsigned IP, unmeetable SLAs, and auto-renewing lock-in. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run a real license scan (npx license-checker --summary or pip-licenses) on a project and paste it into the auditor above — most teams are surprised by what's in their tree. (2) Confirm every contractor who touched core code signed an IP assignment. (3) Read one real SLA or MSA your company signed and find the liability cap and auto-renewal clause. (4) Add automated license scanning to CI so a risky dependency is caught at the PR, not in diligence.
