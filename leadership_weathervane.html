🌤️ The Weathervane

A weathervane doesn't stop the storm — it tells you which way the wind is turning while there's still time to act. Senior leaders are judged less on avoiding every problem than on seeing it coming: naming risks early, keeping stakeholders honestly informed, and raising the alarm at the right moment — neither too late, nor so often that no one listens.

Final module of Stage 3 · Leading Delivery. Hands-on to the end: a real in-browser pre-mortem → risk register is the deliverable — surface a project's risks and score them by likelihood × impact.

1Read the wind before the storm

Junior engineers react to problems; senior leaders anticipate them. A risk is simply an uncertainty that could hurt the outcome — and you can't eliminate risk (a project with no risk isn't worth doing). What you can do is manage it, on a loop: identify the risks, assess each by likelihood and impact, mitigate the ones that matter, and monitor them as things change.

The whole discipline is a bet against the two most expensive words in delivery: "we didn't see it coming." Almost always, someone did — they just had no venue to say it, or no one was watching the vane. Your job is to build both.

2The risk register — likelihood × impact

A risk register is the venue: a living list of what could go wrong, each with a likelihood, an impact, an owner, and a mitigation. Scoring likelihood × impact lets you rank them, because you can't fix everything — you triage. A near-certain catastrophe gets your attention; a rare annoyance does not.

A register is a verb, not a document. The classic failure is writing one at kickoff, filing it, and never looking again — then a listed risk materializes and everyone shrugs "well, it was on the register." Documenting a risk isn't managing it. Review the top risks on a cadence, update the scores as reality shifts, and actually do the mitigations.

3Pre-mortems — imagine the failure

The best way to fill that register is a pre-mortem (Gary Klein's technique), and it's beautifully counterintuitive. A normal "what could go wrong?" brainstorm feels vaguely disloyal to the plan, so people hold back. A pre-mortem removes that friction by making failure a given:

🔮 The pre-mortem prompt: "It's six months from now. This project has failed — completely, embarrassingly. Take five minutes and write down why." Then collect every reason, group them, and turn the biggest into risks with owners and mitigations.

This is "prospective hindsight," and studies find it surfaces roughly 30% more potential problems than the standard framing. Assuming the failure has already happened gives people permission to voice the doubt they've been sitting on — and the quiet, cautious folks (often the ones who see the iceberg) finally speak up. You'll run one in the deliverable below.

4RACI — kill the ownership ambiguity

One of the most common risks on any register is invisible: nobody's sure who owns the decision. Work stalls, everyone assumes someone else has it, and it falls through the cracks. RACI makes ownership explicit for each decision or deliverable:

R — Responsible

Does the actual work. Can be several people.

A — Accountable

Owns the outcome; the buck stops here. Exactly one person, always.

C — Consulted

Gives input before the decision. Two-way conversation.

I — Informed

Told after the decision. One-way, no debate needed.

The one rule that does most of the work: exactly one Accountable per item. "We're all accountable" means no one is. When something's stuck, the fastest unblock is often just naming the single person whose call it is.

5Status execs read — and escalating without crying wolf

Executives skim. A status update that buries the risk in the fourth paragraph, offers no clear status colour, and never asks for anything is a status update no one acts on. Write for a reader who gives you twenty seconds:

❌ Status execs ignore

Buried lede — risk in paragraph 4
A wall of detail, no clear ask
Always "green" (a watermelon)
No status colour up top
"…so, thoughts?"

✅ Status execs read

BLUF — bottom line in line one
Red / Amber / Green at the top
Honest status, even when it's red
One clear "what I need from you"
Skimmable in 20 seconds

The deadliest status is the watermelon: green on the outside, red on the inside — reported healthy right up until it explodes. Leaders can absorb bad news early; they cannot absorb surprises. And escalation is the same discipline in real time: raise risks before they're crises, but calibrate. Escalate too often and you're the boy who cried wolf — your signal gets tuned out. Escalate too late and you own an avoidable disaster. When you do escalate, bring a proposed solution and the specific decision you need, not just a problem dumped on someone's desk.

7Pressure-test your instincts

Risk & stakeholder management fails in predictable ways — skipping the pre-mortem, reporting watermelon-green, mis-calibrating escalation, and leaving ownership vague. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run a real pre-mortem with your team before your next project — five silent minutes of "why did it fail?" beats an hour of confident planning. (2) Score the risks it surfaces and commit to mitigating only the top "act now" ones. (3) Rewrite your next status update BLUF-first with a RAG colour and one clear ask. (4) For your most-stalled decision, name the single Accountable person out loud.
