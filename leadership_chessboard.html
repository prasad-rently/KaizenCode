♟️ The Chessboard

This is where the road arrives at the actual job. A CTO's defining output isn't code or even org design — it's technology strategy: a small set of hard choices about where to invest, where not to, and how technology wins the game the business is playing. A grandmaster doesn't move every piece; they see the board, pick the decisive line, and sacrifice the rest. Strategy is deciding which moves matter.

First module of Stage 5 · The CTO Craft — the summit. The centerpiece is a real strategy-doc builder: write a three-year technology strategy that actually guides decisions.

1Strategy is choosing what NOT to do

The most common thing called "strategy" isn't one. A list of goals ("scale to 10M users, improve reliability, adopt AI, hire great people") is a wish list — every option ticked, nothing sacrificed. Real strategy, as Richard Rumelt frames it, has a hard kernel: a diagnosis of the one challenge that matters most, a guiding policy for how you'll tackle it, and a set of coherent actions that all point the same way. Its defining feature is sacrifice — a strategy that doesn't tell you what to say no to isn't a strategy, it's a mission statement.

❌ Bad strategy (fluff & goals)

A list of goals, all ticked
"Be the best / grow / innovate"
No diagnosis of the real challenge
Nothing is sacrificed or excluded
Changes no one's actual decisions

✅ Good strategy (a hard choice)

Diagnosis of the one thing that matters
A guiding policy to address it
Coherent actions that reinforce each other
An explicit list of what we won't do
Redirects where the money & people go

2A strategy doc that guides decisions

A technology strategy earns its keep only if it changes what people build. The test: six months from now, when a team faces a fork, does the doc tell them which way to go? If it's a shelf-ware slide deck full of aspirations, it fails. A good one is short, opinionated, and referenced constantly — it says here's our bet, here's why, and here's what that means we're not doing. You'll write one in the deliverable, and the discipline is the same as The Storyteller: lead with the choice, justify it, name the trade-offs.

3Moats & defensibility

Strategy asks not just "how do we win?" but "how do we keep winning once competitors notice?" That's a moat — a durable advantage. The hard truth: most technical sophistication is not a moat. Clever architecture and hard engineering feel defensible, but competitors catch up, and being 10% better rarely holds a market. Durable moats are usually structural:

  • Network effects — the product gets better as more people use it (marketplaces, social, platforms). The strongest moat there is.
  • Switching costs — once a customer builds on you, leaving is painful (data, integrations, workflows). Platform APIs create this deliberately.
  • Economies of scale — you can serve cheaper than anyone smaller (infra, data).
  • Data / proprietary assets — a data advantage that compounds and can't be copied.

The strategic question for a CTO: is our technical work building something defensible, or just something impressive? Direct engineering investment toward the moat.

4Build / buy / partner — with real numbers

Every capability you need is a choice: build it, buy it, or partner for it. Engineers reflexively build ("we can do it better"), which quietly spends your scarcest capital — engineering time — on things that don't differentiate you. Decide with the numbers from The Counting House, not ego:

Build

When it's your core differentiator — the thing customers pay you for. Build the moat; own the crown jewels.

Buy

When it's commodity — auth, observability, payments, infra. Someone sells it better and cheaper than you'd build it. Buy speed.

Partner

When you need a capability or reach you can't build fast enough and don't want to own — integrations, certifications, distribution.

The two traps. "Build everything" burns years reinventing commodity infrastructure and starves your differentiator. "Buy everything" leaves you with no moat — a thin wrapper around other people's products that anyone can clone. The strategy is knowing which few things are worth building and ruthlessly buying the rest.

5Innovation portfolio & tech debt as a liability

You can't bet everything on the safe core, or everything on moonshots. A healthy innovation portfolio balances horizons — a common split is roughly 70 / 20 / 10:

70% Core
20% Adjacent
10% Transformational
today's cash cownext marketsthe big bet

Starve the core and today's revenue erodes; skip the 10% and you have no future. As CTO you own that balance — and you defend the 10% when short-term pressure tries to raid it.

Reframe tech debt as a balance-sheet liability, not a moral failing. Like financial debt, it accrues interest — every quarter you don't pay it, everything ships a little slower. Some debt is smart leverage (ship now, refactor later, deliberately); some is reckless (shortcuts nobody chose). Paying it down is an investment in future velocity, and framing it that way (in business terms) is how you win the budget to do it.

6AI strategy — the bet you can't skip

Right now, every CTO owes their company a real answer to "what's our AI strategy?" — and the wrong answers are equally dangerous: AI theater (bolt a chatbot on to look current) and willful blindness (ignore it and hope). A real AI strategy asks the strategic questions: Where does AI genuinely change our product, our cost-to-serve, or our moat? What's now possible that wasn't? What's newly threatened? Then it makes the build-vs-buy call deliberately — for most, you buy the foundation models (the Bedrock/vendor layer) and build the product and data advantage on top. The moat is rarely the model; it's your proprietary data and the workflow you wrap around it.

7Deliverable — a three-year technology strategy

Write a real one for a company you know. Fill the kernel — diagnosis, guiding policy, the big bets, and (crucially) what you won't do — and the builder assembles a strategy one-pager that could actually guide decisions:

Company / context
Diagnosis — the ONE challenge or opportunity that matters most
Guiding policy — our overall approach to it
The 3 big bets (where we invest over 3 years)
What we will NOT do (the sacrifices)
Build / buy / partner calls
How we'll know it's working (the signal)

8Pressure-test your instincts

Strategy fails in predictable ways — mistaking goals for strategy, building what you should buy, trusting a moat that isn't, and doing AI theater. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Write the three-year strategy for a company you know — the hardest and most revealing field is "what we will NOT do." (2) For your current work, ask of each major effort: build, buy, or partner — and is engineering time going to the moat? (3) Name your company's real moat honestly; if it's "our tech is better," keep looking. (4) Write one sentence on where AI genuinely changes your product — no theater.
