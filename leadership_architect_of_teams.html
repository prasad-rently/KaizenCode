🏗️ The Architect of Teams

At some point you stop designing software and start designing the system that designs the software — the teams, their boundaries, and how they talk. It's the highest-leverage architecture you'll ever draw, because the shape of your org quietly becomes the shape of your product. Get it right and work flows; get it wrong and no amount of individual talent rescues it.

Third module of Stage 3 · Leading Delivery. Hands-on again: a real in-browser org-design calculator is the deliverable — see how teams, layers and managers should scale at 10 → 30 → 100 engineers.

1Org design is system design — Conway's Law

In 1967 Melvin Conway observed that organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure. It's held up remarkably well: four teams that don't talk will build four services that don't integrate; one big undifferentiated team will build one big undifferentiated ball of mud. Your architecture diagram and your org chart converge whether you intend it or not.

The leverage move is the Inverse Conway Maneuver: instead of fighting this, design your teams to match the architecture you want. Want clean, independently deployable services? Give each one a team that owns it end-to-end. You don't enforce good boundaries with rules and reviews — you enforce them by drawing the team lines where you want the system lines.

2Team Topologies — the shapes of teams

The book Team Topologies (Skelton & Pais) gives the modern vocabulary. Almost every team should be one of four types — and the great majority should be the first:

Stream-aligned

Owns a slice of the product end-to-end (a user journey, a domain). The default — most teams are these, and everything else exists to serve them.

Platform

Provides internal services and "paved roads" (CI, infra, auth) that let stream teams go fast without reinventing the plumbing.

Enabling

Specialists who temporarily help a stream team pick up a new capability (testing, security) — then leave. Coaches, not owners.

Complicated-subsystem

A deep-expertise team for a genuinely hard component (a codec, an ML core) where scattered ownership would fail.

Two rules make it work. Keep teams small and long-lived — a stable "two-pizza" team that owns its area for years beats project teams that form and dissolve. And respect cognitive load: a team should own only as much as it can actually hold in its collective head. Overload a team with unrelated responsibilities and everything slows down, no matter how strong the individuals.

3Spans, layers & managing managers

Two numbers shape the management structure. Span of control = how many people report to one manager; the healthy range is roughly 5–8. Too wide and people get neglected (no real 1:1s, no growth); too narrow and you get micromanagement and needless layers. Layers = how many management levels deep the org is; keep it as flat as you responsibly can, because every layer adds distance, distortion, and delay between the top and the work.

When a span gets too wide, you add a layer — and cross a threshold: you start managing managers. This is its own step-change, as big as IC→manager was:

  • Your leverage is now other people's leadership. You succeed through managers who succeed through their teams — two levels removed from the code.
  • Coach judgment, not tasks. You're no longer assigning work; you're developing decision-makers. Resist solving their teams' problems for them — it undermines them and doesn't scale.
  • Use skip-level 1:1s to keep ground truth: talk directly with your managers' reports occasionally, so you're not seeing the org only through a filtered layer.

4Platform vs product teams

Early on, every team does its own infra, and that's fine. As you grow, the same plumbing gets rebuilt five times and drifts five different ways — that's the signal to carve out a platform team. But there's a classic failure mode waiting: the platform team becomes a ticket queue that everyone waits on, and turns from an accelerator into the bottleneck.

Run platform as a product, not a gatekeeper. Its customers are internal engineers; its job is self-service tools and paved roads they can consume without waiting for a ticket. Measure it by adoption and internal satisfaction, not tickets closed. The moment stream teams have to queue behind it, you've recreated the silo you were trying to remove.

5Reorgs that don't destroy trust

Reorganizations are powerful and expensive. Every reorg resets forming-storming-norming, severs hard-won working relationships, and burns weeks of context and morale. That cost is worth paying occasionally for a real structural reason — and never worth paying for a fake one.

❌ Reorg as avoidance

Used to route around a difficult person
No clear "why" anyone can repeat
Frequent — every few months
Sprung as a surprise
Breaks teams that were working well

✅ Reorg done right

Solves a real structural / flow problem
A crisp rationale, communicated openly
Rare, and given time to settle
Explained early, with the reasoning
Protects bonds; disrupts only what must change

The most common bad reorg is the one used to avoid a hard conversation — reshuffling an entire group because of one difficult individual you haven't managed directly. Deal with the person (that's The Diplomat and The Referee); don't inflict a reorg on everyone to dodge it.

7Pressure-test your instincts

Org design fails in predictable ways — architecture fighting the org chart, spans stretched past breaking, reorgs used to dodge people problems, and platform teams turned into bottlenecks. Pick what you'd actually do, then see how it plays out:

Try it: (1) Run the calculator at your current headcount — is your real span inside 5–8, and your layer count as flat as it could be? (2) Sketch the architecture you want, then check whether your team boundaries match it (inverse Conway). (3) Label each of your teams with a Team-Topologies type — any team that's secretly three types is overloaded. (4) If you've reorged in the last year, ask honestly: was it structural, or was it avoiding a conversation?
